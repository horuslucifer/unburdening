What the records say

The records keep their own clocks, and this year can be set on all of them. On the count of years from Adam that a modern chronologer, Jason Breshears, takes from Steven Jones, 2026 is the year 5920; on the Hebrew calendar it is 5787; the Indian astronomer Āryabhaṭa counted his own era from "the Thursday of the Bhārata battle"; and Plato's Egyptian priest, who told Solon "you Hellenes are never anything but children", counted nine thousand years back to the war with Atlantis. The Etruscans held that their nation had been given a set number of ages, each ended by a sign from heaven; Censorinus, writing in AD 238, defines the measure: "A natural cycle or age is the longest duration of human life, having for its limits the birth and death of man." The dates the records have already fixed are kept here: Mher's door opens next on the eve of Ascension, 6 May 2027 by one Easter reckoning or 10 June by the other; and Venus, the morning star, whose timetable the companion poem to this volume, The Taking Down, follows, passes between the earth and the sun on 24 October 2026 and returns at dawn on the thirtieth. The Erra poem ends with a promise to the house that keeps its tablet: "The house in which this tablet is placed" will be spared the plague. This volume is that kind of record. It keeps what the archives say, marks what it joined, and hands it on.
