I. The Elders

Before this chapter comes the prologue, The Horse's Foot, which asked how the ground came to be so heavy. This chapter goes back to the beings that the records place under that ground. Two signs are used throughout. Where we, and no old source, make the link between two archives, the text says the link is ours and marks it ◇. The word burden is this book's own name for the weight its story follows; it comes from a Hittite curse told in this chapter, and a later chapter, The Burden, is named for it. The line under each paragraph that begins with ~ gives the sources, and the volume's Names table gives each figure's names in every language.

Many peoples' oldest records begin with someone who was there before their gods. In most of them the younger gods throw that elder down and take the rule, and in most of them the elder survives his fall. The records keep him in four ways, and the chapter ends by gathering them: he sleeps, bound, under the earth or on an island at the edge of the world; he is sworn by in the oaths of the kings who came after; he is cut up and made into the ground the living walk on; or he keeps a knowledge that the younger have to come to him for. The chapter tells the elders by what happens to them, so that the peoples who tell the same thing stand side by side: the Norse, the Greeks, the Babylonians, the Indians and the Maya, and others less known today, such as the Hittites of old Turkey and the Phoenicians of the coast of Lebanon.

Before anything had a name

Babylon's poem of creation is called Enūma eliš after its first two words, which mean when on high. It was composed in Akkadian, the language of Babylonia and Assyria, in the second millennium BC, and the priests of Babylon recited it at their New Year festival. It opens on a time when nothing had a name. In L. W. King's English of 1902 it begins "When in the height heaven was not named," and goes on, "And the earth beneath did not yet bear a name". There were only two waters, mingled together: Apsû, the sweet water under the earth, and Tiamat, the salt sea. The gods were born inside them, generation after generation, and the first gods in the poem are the children of water.

Enūma eliš I 1-2, tr. King 1902

The Greeks knew the same beginning under Greek spellings. Damascius, the last head of Plato's school at Athens in the sixth century AD, wrote that the Babylonians "constitute Two, Tauthe and Apason; making Apason the husband of Tauthe, and denominating her the mother of the gods." Tauthe is Tiamat and Apason is Apsû, and Damascius follows the gods born of them down the generations in nearly the same order as the poem.

Damascius, De principiis 125, in Cory 1832

Genesis, the first book of the Hebrew Bible, also opens on darkness and water, and its word for the deep, tehom, is of the same family as Tiamat's name; but in Genesis the deep is only water, there to be divided, and nobody is born of it. Genesis gives its God no parents, no elder and no rival, and nothing that was there before him. The likeness of the two words is a matter of language; setting the deep of Genesis beside the mother of Babylon's gods is ours ◇.

At the other side of the world the Hawaiian chant of the chiefs called the Kumulipo, sixteen eras long, begins in the night. King Kalākaua printed it in Hawaiian in 1889, and his sister, Queen Liliʻuokalani, put it into English while she was a prisoner in 1895, after her kingdom was overthrown. The Hawaiian runs "Po wale ho'i / Hanau ka po", and the Queen's English "It is night, / So was night born." Then "Kumulipo was born in the night, a male. / Poele was born in the night, a female." Through the eras of the night the chant brings out creatures of the sea and the land, and a refrain keeps the door shut behind them: "So the gods may enter, but not man." The gods themselves come late, at line 601, with the words "Hanau ka po Akua", in our word-for-word rendering the night bore gods.

Kumulipo 11-14, 601 (Kalākaua 1889) · tr. Liliuokalani 1897, pp. 1-2

The Māori of New Zealand told it the same way. Sir George Grey, governor of the colony, wrote down what Māori elders told him and printed it in 1855: "Rangi and Papa, or Heaven and Earth, were the source from which, in the beginning, all things originated." They lay clasped together in the dark, and the dark was counted out in nights: "these divisions of times were considered as beings, and were each termed a Po". The Hawaiian pō and the Māori Po are the same word, night, in two languages of one family; neither Grey nor the Queen compares the two chants, and the comparison is ours ◇.

Grey, Polynesian Mythology, pp. 1-2, 1855

The Buddhists of India tell it as water and dark at the turning of a world. The Aggañña Sutta, a discourse of the Buddha on what is first known, in the Long Discourses of the Pali canon (the Buddhist scriptures written down in Sri Lanka in the first century BC), says: "Now at that time, all had become one world of water, dark, and of darkness that maketh blind." There was no sun, no month and no season, and "Beings were reckoned just as beings only." What those beings did next, coming down from a world of light, belongs to the next chapter.

Aggañña Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 27.11, tr. Rhys Davids 1921

In Iran, before the world was made, there were already two. The Gāthās are hymns that the Zoroastrian tradition gives to its prophet Zarathustra, in the oldest form of Avestan, the sacred language of the Zoroastrians; scholars date them anywhere from the second millennium BC to the first. One of them speaks of "the primeval spirits who as a pair" stand at the beginning, opposite in what they strive for and each acting on its own. L. H. Mills, who translated the hymns in the 1880s, gives the bare word order in a note, and there the pair are "two-twins". Every later choice between good and evil, in the Zoroastrian telling, goes back to those two.

Yasna 30.3, tr. Mills (SBE)

Mani, a prophet born in Babylonia in AD 216, founded a church of his own that once reached from North Africa to China, and he took up Iran's two first principles as light and dark. He taught three moments. In the first there was "before heaven and earth, only light and dark distinct". In the middle moment "darkness having invaded the light gives rein to its passions; light entering the darkness gives up its substance and is shifted about", and at the end the two are parted again.

the Chinese Compendium of Mani's teaching (731), English after Haloun and Henning 1952, as Unit 5 gives it, our rendering

The eldest, and the title refused

The Norse remember the eldest by name. Snorri Sturluson, the Christian Icelander who about 1220 wrote down the old poems and stories in the book now called his Edda, tells it as a conversation: a Swedish king called Gylfi comes in disguise to the hall of the gods and asks three figures on three thrones how the world began. They tell him of a frost-giant who lived in the void before there was earth or sky, and they refuse him the title of god: "By no means do we acknowledge him God; he was evil and all his kindred: we call them Rime-Giants." Rime is hoar-frost. The chapter ends with his name: "The old Rime-Giant, him we call Ymir."

Gylfaginning 5, tr. Brodeur 1916

The poems Snorri drew on were older than his book. In the Vafþrúðnismál, the lay of Vafþrúðnir, the god Odin goes in disguise to the hall of a wise giant of that name and wagers his head in a contest of knowledge. Among his questions is the one this chapter turns on: which of the gods or of Ymir's kin was eldest in the early days. The giant's answer goes back to Ymir. The eldest giant he can name is Bergelmir, born "Winters unmeasured ere earth was made", the son of Thruthgelmir and the grandson of Aurgelmir; and Aurgelmir, Snorri explains, is the giants' own name for Ymir. Ymir is the root of the whole giant line. In the Völuspá, the Prophecy of the Seeress, the woman who tells Odin the world's whole story begins with the elders who raised her: "I remember yet the giants of yore, / Who gave me bread in the days gone by;"

Vafþrúðnismál 28-29, tr. Bellows 1923 · Völuspá 2, tr. Bellows 1923

The same refusal stands in a Buddhist discourse, the Pāṭika Sutta. When a world begins again, the palace of Brahmā appears "but it is empty", and the first being to fall into it from the World of Radiance lives there alone and longs for company. When others arrive after him he takes himself for their maker: "I am Brahmā, the great Brahmā, the Vanquisher, the Unvanquished, the All-Seeing ... By me are these beings created." The newcomers agree, for a reason they give themselves: "Because he, as we see, arose here first, but we arose after him." The discourse lets him keep his place as the first and denies him the claim to have made the rest. Snorri keeps Ymir as the first and denies him the name of god. The two traditions never met, and reading the one refusal in both is ours ◇. The Hebrew Bible has its own first one who claimed too much, the king of Babylon in Isaiah who said he would be like the Most High; that telling belongs to a later chapter, Claimed.

Pāṭika Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 24.2.15-17, tr. Rhys Davids 1921

Other northern peoples kept an elder who was born out of the earth or born old. Tacitus, the Roman historian, wrote in AD 98 that the Germans in their songs "celebrate the god Tuisto, sprung from the earth, and his son Mannus, as the fathers and founders of their race." The Finnish Kalevala, the epic Elias Lönnrot gathered from the songs of Finland and Karelia in the 1830s and 1840s, has its singer and sage come into a world already made, but he is the elder of everyone in it, born already old after thirty years in the womb: "Vainamoinen, old and steadfast, / Rested in his mother's body / For the space of thirty summers, / And the sum of thirty winters". Ireland's Book of Invasions, the Lebor Gabála Érenn of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, quoting a lost older book, gives Banba, one of the three goddesses whose names are names of Ireland, the claim to have been in Ireland before the Flood: "I am elder than Noe... upon this mountain was I in the Flood". Noe is Noah.

Tacitus, Germania 2, Oxford translation · Kalevala 1.287-292, tr. Kirby 1907 · Lebor Gabála Érenn ¶438, tr. Macalister

Three kings in heaven

The Hittites ruled central Anatolia, the land that is now Turkey, from their capital Hattusa in the second millennium BC, and their scribes copied into Hittite the songs of their neighbours the Hurrians, a people of northern Syria and Mesopotamia. One of these, the Song of Kumarbi, which its modern editors call Kingship in Heaven, tells of three kings of heaven, each thrown down by the next. It begins "Once in the olden days Alalus was king in heaven." Alalu sat on the throne and Anu, the sky, stood before him as his cupbearer. "Nine in number were the years that Alalus was king in heaven." In the ninth year Anu fought him, and Alalu fled down into the dark earth.

Song of Kumarbi, CTH 344, tr. Goetze, ANET p. 120

Then the same thing happened to Anu. Kumarbi, of Alalu's line, served him as Anu had served Alalu, and in the ninth year fought him. Anu fled up toward the sky, and Kumarbi caught him by the feet, pulled him down and bit off his loins, swallowing his manhood. Anu's answer is the curse of a deposed king who knows what he has left behind him: "In thine inside I have planted a heavy burden." He names three gods now growing in Kumarbi, the first of them the Storm-god, who in the songs that follow becomes the next king of heaven. Kumarbi, who swallowed his elder's seed, carries the younger who will throw him down.

Song of Kumarbi, CTH 344, tr. Goetze, ANET; tr. Rieken

The deposed do not vanish in these songs. Alalu went down into the dark earth, and in one reading of the later songs Kumarbi too makes his home under the earth, where he goes on plotting against the Storm-god. In the Song of Ullikummi he fathers a giant of stone to take back the throne, and the stone grows on the shoulder of Ubelluri, a giant who carries the world and never noticed: "When they built heaven and earth upon me I did not know anything." To cut the stone giant loose the gods open the storehouse of the elder gods and bring out "the former copper saw", the old tool with which heaven was once severed from earth. The elders' tools are kept below, and they still cut.

Song of Ullikummi, CTH 345, tr. Goetze, ANET · Hittite karuili, the former

The Hittite kings went on swearing by the losers. Their treaties close with long lists of gods called to witness, and near the end of the lists, just before the mountains and rivers, stand the old gods of the songs. A treaty that the Hittite king Muwattalli II made about 1280 BC calls on "all the primeval deities ... Alalu, Kumarbi" with the mountains and rivers of Hatti and the great sea, and an earlier treaty, with the king of Amurru on the Syrian coast, calls the whole list of olden gods "these gods of the oath". The kings who had been thrown down in heaven were the ones a living king called to hear his oath. The partner in that later treaty was Alaksandu, king of Wilusa in the north-west of Anatolia, and many modern scholars read Wilusa as Ilios, the city the Greeks called Troy, and Alaksandu as Alexandros, the other name of Troy's prince Paris.

Treaty of Muwattalli II with Alaksandu of Wilusa, CTH 76, tr. Beckman · Treaty of Muršili II with Tuppi-Teššub of Amurru, CTH 62, tr. Goetze, ANET

At Ugarit, a trading city on the Syrian coast whose clay tablets of the thirteenth century BC were dug up after 1929, the father of the gods was El, a grey-bearded king who sat in council at the springs of the rivers. The scribes of Ugarit kept god-lists, tables that set the names of one people's gods beside another people's, line by line, and on El's line they wrote the Hurrian Kumarbi (and, in the Sumerian column, Enlil): one god under several peoples' names.

the polyglot god-list of Ugarit, as SAFAH Act IV and Unit 8 give it

Heaven, his son and his son's son

The Greeks told the same succession through three generations. Hesiod, a poet of Boeotia in central Greece, about 700 BC, sang it in the Theogony, the Birth of the Gods. Heaven, Ouranos in Greek, lay on Earth, and would not let their children be born into the light: "And he used to hide them all away in a secret place of Earth so soon as each was born, and would not suffer them to come up into the light: and Heaven rejoiced in his evil doing. But vast Earth groaned within, being straitened, and she made the element of grey flint and shaped a great sickle, and told her plan to her dear sons." The youngest, Kronos, took the sickle and cut his father, and Heaven drew back from Earth. (The older English translations quoted here spell him Cronos or Cronus; in Latin he is Saturn.) Heaven named his sons for what they had done: "great Heaven used to call Titans (Strainers) in reproach, for he said that they strained and did presumptuously a fearful deed, and that vengeance for it would come afterwards."

Hesiod, Theogony 156-166, 207-210, tr. Evelyn-White 1914

The vengeance came by the same road. Kronos learned "that he was destined to be overcome by his own son, strong though he was, through the contriving of great Zeus", and he swallowed his children as they were born, until their mother Rhea gave him a stone in place of the last of them, Zeus. Zeus grew up hidden, made his father bring the swallowed children up again, and "set free from their deadly bonds the brothers of his father, sons of Heaven whom his father in his foolishness had bound." Heaven had bound his own sons, Kronos's brothers; Zeus loosed them and used them against Kronos. After ten years of war with the Titans the victors "pressed far-seeing Olympian Zeus to reign and to rule over them, by Earth’s prompting." Earth stands behind every throw: she gave Kronos the sickle and gave Zeus the counsel.

Hesiod, Theogony 463-465, 501-502, 881-883, tr. Evelyn-White 1914

Hesiod measures where the losers went. Tartarus is as far below the earth as the earth is below heaven: "a brazen anvil falling down from heaven nine nights and days would reach the earth upon the tenth", and nine more down to Tartarus. "There by the counsel of Zeus who drives the clouds the Titan gods are hidden under misty gloom, in a dank place where are the ends of the huge earth. And they may not go out; for Poseidon fixed gates of bronze upon it". Their guards are the hundred-handed giants their father Heaven had once bound: "There Gyes and Cottus and great-souled Obriareus live, trusty warders of Zeus who holds the aegis." The Hittite song counts nine years to each of its first two kings of heaven, and Hesiod counts nine days of falling to each floor of the world; nobody in the records joins the two nines, and the joining is ours ◇.

Hesiod, Theogony 722-735, tr. Evelyn-White 1914

Homer, whose Iliad was sung in the Greek cities of Asia Minor in the eighth or seventh century BC, knew the elders as still there below, out of the light. Zeus threatens a disobedient god with Tartarus, "where abide Iapetus and Cronos, and have joy neither in the rays of Helios Hyperion nor in any breeze, but deep Tartarus is round about them." Iapetus is a Titan, a brother of Kronos and the father of Prometheus.

Iliad 8.478-481, tr. Murray 1924

The Titans' side of it was told too. The Prometheus Bound, a tragedy staged at Athens in the fifth century BC under the name of Aeschylus, gives the voice to Prometheus, a Titan's son nailed by Zeus to a rock in Scythia, at the edge of the world, who speaks of the new reign as a coup: "For new rulers lord it in heaven, and with new-fangled laws Zeus wieldeth arbitrary sway; and that which was mighty of old he now bringeth to naught." He also knows that the old king's curse is still running, and that one day Zeus too will fall, "and then shall straightway be fulfilled to the uttermost the malison his father Cronus imprecated as he fell from his ancient throne." The elder thrown down leaves behind him a curse with a term on it.

Prometheus Bound 148-151, 907-912, tr. Smyth 1922

A Latin telling gave the quarrel to two brothers. Ennius, a Roman poet of the second century BC, put into Latin the Sacred History of Euhemerus, a Greek of about 300 BC who wrote that the gods had once been kings on earth. Lactantius, a Christian teacher of about AD 300, preserves the passage. In this telling Titan is one person, Saturn's elder brother, where in Hesiod the Titans are the whole family: "Titan, who was older than Saturn, demands the kingdom for himself." Their mother and sisters took Saturn's side, and Titan yielded on a bargain: "He therefore made an agreement with Saturn, that if any male children should be born to him, he would not bring them up." Here the swallowing of the children is a debt owed to the elder brother.

Ennius in Lactantius, Divine Institutes 1.14, tr. Fletcher (ANF)

The fallen king is also remembered as the best one. Hesiod's Works and Days says the gods first made "a golden race of mortal men who lived in the time of Cronos when he was reigning in heaven." Plato, the Athenian philosopher of the fourth century BC, tells in the Laws how Kronos, knowing that no man can hold power over men without growing insolent, gave them better rulers: "Which reflection led him to appoint not men but demigods, who are of a higher and more divine race, to be the kings and rulers of our cities". Virgil, the poet of Rome, in the Aeneid of about 19 BC, sends the deposed king to Italy, where the old king Evander tells Aeneas: "First from heavenly Olympus came Saturn, fleeing from the weapons of Jove and exiled from his lost realm." Jove is Jupiter, the Latin Zeus. Saturn gave the wild hill-people laws, "and chose that the land be called Latium, since in these borders he had found a safe hiding-place. Under his reign were the golden ages men tell of". The Latin makes a pun of it: Latium, the land where he lay hidden, latere in Latin.

Hesiod, Works and Days 109-111, tr. Evelyn-White 1914 · Plato, Laws 713c-d, tr. Jowett 1892 · Aeneid 8.319-325, tr. Fairclough 1918

John Milton, the English poet, wrote the whole succession into Paradise Lost in 1667 as a line of usurpers. He counts the Greek gods among the fallen angels who took the names of the nations' gods, "Gods, yet confessed later than Heaven and Earth, / Their boasted parents;—Titan, Heaven's first-born, / With his enormous brood, and birthright seized / By younger Saturn: he from mightier Jove, / His own and Rhea's son, like measure found; / So Jove usurping reigned." Titan as the first-born and Saturn as the younger brother who seized his birthright is the Ennius telling above; that Milton took it from Ennius is our guess ◇.

Paradise Lost 1.509-514, Milton 1667

Philo's Phoenicians, and the names laid over names

Philo of Byblos, a Phoenician who wrote in Greek about AD 100, said he had translated the book of an older Phoenician sage, Sanchuniathon. Philo's work is lost except where Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine about 313, copied it into his Preparation for the Gospel to show that the pagans' gods had been men. Philo lays Greek names over Phoenician ones as he goes, and the one to hold on to is this: the Phoenician El, the father of the gods at Ugarit, is the Greek Kronos. Philo says so outright. Put that beside the god-lists of Ugarit, which write Kumarbi, the Hittite song's king who bit off Anu's loins, on El's line, and there is a chain from Kumarbi through El to Kronos: three names for one god who overthrew his father the Sky. Neither source makes the whole chain, and the chain is ours ◇.

Philo's succession has four generations. At its head is "a certain Elioun called "the Most High," and a female named Beruth, and these dwelt in the neighbourhood of Byblos." Their son was afterwards called Uranus, the Greek for sky, and gave his name to it, and his sister gave hers, Ge, to the earth. The Most High "died in an encounter with wild beasts, and was deified".

Philo of Byblos in Eusebius, PE 1.10.14-15, tr. Gifford 1903

Uranus married his sister, and the first of their sons was "Elus who is also Kronos". Kronos, with the counsel of Hermes, who in Philo is his secretary, "repels his father Uranus, and avenges his mother." He builds a wall round his house "and founds the first city, Byblos in Phoenicia." He throws his brother Atlas into a deep pit and buries him there, and "the allies of Elus, who is Kronos, were surnamed Eloim". Eloim is Philo's spelling of the Hebrew Elohim, the word the Hebrew Bible uses for its one God; giving the name to Kronos's allies is Philo's doing.

Philo of Byblos in Eusebius, PE 1.10.16-20, tr. Gifford 1903

After thirty-two years of his reign came the end of the elder: "Elus, that is Kronos, having waylaid his father Uranus in an inland spot, and got him into his hands, emasculates him near some fountains and rivers. There Uranus was deified: and as he breathed his last, the blood from his wounds dropped into the fountains and into the waters of the rivers, and the spot is pointed out to this day." The elder in Philo dies into the ground and the water, is worshipped where he fell, and the place is still shown. Philo's Kronos also carried emblems that fit a king who keeps watch over a deposed line: four eyes and four wings, because "Kronos was watchful even when in repose, and was in repose even when awake".

Philo of Byblos in Eusebius, PE 1.10.29, tr. Gifford 1903; PE 1.10.37, tr. Attridge and Oden

Diodorus of Sicily, a Greek historian of the first century BC, told a version on the edge of the western ocean. His Atlantians, a people by the Atlantic, had as their first king Uranus, who gathered men into cities and watched the stars so well that after his death they gave him divine honours and called the sky by his name; his sons divided the kingdom, and "Cronus, the brother of Atlas, the myth continues, who was a man notorious for his impiety and greed, married his sister Rhea". In Diodorus, as in Philo, the gods are kings first and gods after.

Diodorus 3.56, 3.61.1, tr. Oldfather (Loeb 1935)

Philo's first god, Elioun the Most High, has a likeness in Genesis 14, where Melchizedek, king of Salem, is priest of El Elyon, God Most High, and blesses Abraham in his name. Philo does not cite Genesis and Genesis does not know Philo's Elioun; the likeness of the names is ours to point out ◇, and Genesis's Most High has neither father nor successor.

The Sibyl's Kronos, and Time in Armenia

The Sibyl was a prophetess of Greek legend, and Jews and Christians wrote books of oracles in her name. The third book of the Sibylline Oracles, written by Jews in Egypt in the second or first century BC, tells the Greek succession as history, after the flood and the tower of Babel: "And then the generation tenth appeared / Of mortal men, from the time when the flood / Came upon earlier men. And Cronos reigned, / And Titan and Iapetus; and men called them / Best offspring of Gaia and of Uranus, / Giving to them names both of earth and heaven. / Since they were very first of mortal men." In the Sibyl the elders are men, the first men after the flood, and the earth was shared among them by lot, "for there were oaths of the father and just portions".

Sibylline Oracles 3.108-114, tr. Terry 1899; 3.114-121, the source's gloss

In the Sibyl the binding is turned round. Titan was the elder brother, and when he learned that Cronos had hidden sons, "Titan sixty youths / Together gathered, and held fast in chains / Cronos and his wife Rhea, and concealed / Them in the earth and guarded them in bonds". Cronos's sons rose to free him: "And this is the beginning of dire war / Among all mortals." At the end of it "did God award the Titans evil. / And all of Titans and of Cronos born / Died." The Sibyl's Kronos is bound by his elder and buried alive in the earth, the place Hesiod gives to the Titans. Abydenus, a Greek writer on Assyria known only through later quotations, also put the war after the tower: when the gods had thrown it down and confused men's tongues, "a war arose between Cronus and Titan."

Sibylline Oracles 3.147-155, tr. Terry 1899 · Abydenus in Eusebius, in Cory 1832

In Armenia and Persia the eldest god became Time. The Armenians took the Sibyl's kings into their own history. Movsēs Khorenatsʻi, whom Armenian tradition places in the fifth century AD, begins his History of the Armenians with the Sibyl, after the flood. Its hero in the Babylonian telling is Xisuthros, who like Noah rode out the flood in a boat, and whose story the Babylonian priest Berossus put into Greek (Berossus comes back below): "Before the tower, and before the voice of the human race became many-tongued, and after the voyage of Xisuthros ... to Armenia, Zruan and Titan and Yapetostʻē were princes of the earth." Where the Sibyl has Kronos, Movsēs has Zruan, and Zruan is Zurvan, the Iranian god of Time. Movsēs says another book, found in the royal archive at Nineveh, told it this way: "Terrible and eminent were the first of the gods, and causes of the great benefits of the world, the beginning of the earth and of the multiplication [of men]. From these was separated the race of the giants". The giants, and the giants of Genesis 6 in the days before the flood, belong to a later chapter, Hybrids.

Movsēs Khorenatsʻi 1.6, 1.9, our rendering · his source identified by P. Vetter 1892; Movsēs set among the witnesses to the third Sibylline book by J. Geffcken 1902

The name Kronos sounds almost the same as chronos, the Greek word for time, and the writers here run the two together. The Armenian translation of Eusebius's Chronicle says that Kron is the one some call the father of Aramazd and others call Time. Aramazd is the Armenian form of Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord of the Zoroastrians. The Greeks had heard the same of the Persian sages. Damascius reports, after Eudemus of Rhodes, a pupil of Aristotle in the fourth century BC, that some of the Magi, the Persian priests, call the first of all things Place, "while others call it Time (Chronus): from whom separately proceed a Good Divinity and an Evil Dæmon". So in Persia the eldest is Time, and the two first spirits are his sons.

Damascius, after Eudemus, in Cory 1832

The Zoroastrian books themselves give the contest of the two spirits a term. The Bundahishn, the Creation, a Middle Persian book put together in the ninth or tenth century AD from older teaching, has Ohrmazd (the later form of the name Ahura Mazda) offer peace, and the evil spirit refuse it. Ohrmazd then names a time limit: "Appoint a period! so that the intermingling of the conflict may be for nine thousand years." The evil spirit agrees, "just like two men quarrelling together, who propose a time thus: Let us appoint such-and-such a day for a fight." The Zoroastrian books divide those nine thousand years into three ages of three thousand. Plutarch, the Greek priest and essayist of about AD 100, had a like reckoning in three-thousand-year turns from Theopompus, a historian of the fourth century BC: "one god is to overpower, and the other to be overpowered, each in turn for the space of three thousand years". Reading his turns and the Bundahishn's nine thousand years as one count is ours ◇.

Bundahishn 1.18-19, tr. West (SBE) · Plutarch, On Isis and Osiris 47, tr. Babbitt (Loeb)

The elder's body made into the world

What becomes of the elder's body, in many of these records, is the world. In Enūma eliš the young god Marduk, the god of Babylon, fights Tiamat and kills her, and then, in King's words, "He split her up like a flat fish into two halves". Berossus, a priest of Babylon who wrote its history in Greek about 290 BC, tells the same thing to the Greeks. He describes the monstrous creatures of the first waters, and then the one who ruled them: "The person, who presided over them, was a woman named Omoroca; which in the Chaldæan language is Thalatth; in Greek Thalassa, the sea; but which might equally be interpreted the Moon." Then "Belus came, and cut the woman asunder: and of one half of her he formed the earth, and of the other half the heavens". Belus is Bēl, Lord, the title of Marduk, and Berossus adds his own Greek equation: "This Belus, by whom they signify Jupiter". Omoroca and Thalatth are Berossus's names for Tiamat, as Tauthe is Damascius's.

Enūma eliš IV 137, tr. King 1902 · Berossus, in Cory 1832

The Norse say the earth was made of Ymir. The sons of Borr, Odin and his two brothers, killed him, and so much blood ran out of him that it drowned the Rime-Giants, "save that one, whom giants call Bergelmir, escaped with his household; he went upon his ship, and his wife with him, and they were safe there. And from them are come the races of the Rime-Giants". Then "They took Ymir and bore him into the middle of the Yawning Void, and made of him the earth: of his blood the sea and the waters; the land was made of his flesh, and the crags of his bones". The poem says it in verse: "Out of Ymir's flesh was fashioned the earth, / And the mountains were made of his bones; / The sky from the frost-cold giant's skull, / And the ocean out of his blood." Even the wall of the human world came from him: the gods "raised up the brows of Ymir the giant, and called that place Midgard."

Gylfaginning 7-8, tr. Brodeur 1916 · Vafþrúðnismál 21, tr. Bellows 1923

The two bodies were set side by side in the nineteenth century. George Stanley Faber, an English clergyman who wrote on the origins of pagan religion in 1816, took Ymer, as he spelled it, to be Omoroca in a simpler form, so that the giant cut up to make the Norse earth and the woman cut in two to make the Babylonian earth and sky would carry one name. The equation is his.

G. S. Faber 1816, through Mallet's French Edda, as Unit 7 gives it

The Māori tell it as a separation. Rangi the Sky and Papa the Earth lay clasped, and their children lived in the dark between them. The children held a council, and one of them, Tu-matauenga, the fiercest, said kill them. Another, Tane-mahuta, father of the forests, said "Nay, not so. It is better to rend them apart, and to let the heaven stand far above us, and the earth lie under our feet. Let the sky become as a stranger to us, but the earth remain close to us as our nursing mother." Tane set his head on his mother and his feet against his father and pushed them apart, and they cried out: "Wherefore slay you thus your parents? Why commit you so dreadful a crime as to slay us, as to rend your parents apart?" Only one of the brothers took the parents' side. Tawhiri-ma-tea, god of winds and storms, went up to live with his father, and he is the storm that attacks his brothers and mankind still.

Grey, Polynesian Mythology, pp. 2-6, 1855

Sumer told of an elder who was turned to stone. The Sumerian poem Lugal-e tells how the warrior god Ninurta fought Asag, a monster born of Earth that ruled the mountains, of whom it is said "it actually decides the Land's lawsuits, just as you do", a rival judge to the gods. When Ninurta had beaten it he took its name away: "do not say Asag: its name shall be Stone." The defeated one became the stone of the mountains.

Lugal-e 35-53, 327, ETCSL

Men made from a god's blood

Some records put the elders' fall at the root of mankind. The Babylonian poem of Atra-ḫasīs, the Exceedingly Wise, copied about 1650 BC, begins before there were men: "When gods were man, / They did forced labor, they bore drudgery." The line means that the gods stood where men stand now and did men's work. "The great Anunna-gods, the seven, were burdening / The Igigi-gods with forced labor." The Anunna were the senior gods and the Igigi the junior ones, who dug the rivers for generations until they rebelled. In the night they surrounded the house of Enlil, the lord of the earth, set fire to their tools and declared war on him. The gods' assembly answered with a new creature, made to take the gods' drudgery on itself. One god, We-ila, was slaughtered, and the birth-goddess mixed his flesh and blood with clay, "From the flesh of the god let a spirit remain," so that men would carry a slain god's spirit in them.

Atra-ḫasīs OB I 1-6, 61-73, 190-230, tr. Foster (eBL)

Enūma eliš tells the same making with a different god killed. After Tiamat's fall, her captain Qingu was brought to judgment, bound and cut: "From his blood they (Marduk and Ea) made humankind," to take the gods' toil on themselves. Berossus has a different telling, in which the god gives his own head for it: "the deity above-mentioned took off his own head: upon which the other gods mixed the blood, as it gushed out, with the earth; and from thence were formed men. On this account it is that they are rational, and partake of divine knowledge."

Enūma eliš VI 33, tr. Foster (eBL); King 1902, before the Qingu lines were known, read VI 5 as Marduk's own blood · Berossus, in Cory 1832

In the Hittite song Anu planted a heavy burden inside the elder king, and in Atra-ḫasīs the senior gods were burdening the junior ones with work until it was laid on men. The Hittite text has its own word for that weight and the Babylonian another; this book calls both the burden, and the joining of the two weights is ours ◇. A later chapter, The Burden, follows that weight onto the earth.

The elder brothers

In India the elder gods have a name of their own, the asuras, and the Indian books say plainly that they were the older brothers. The Mahābhārata, the Sanskrit epic of India's great war, composed over many centuries around the turn of the eras, has the sage Vyāsa console Yudhiṣṭhira, the eldest of the five brothers who won the war, for having killed his own kin. He reminds him of the war "of gods and asuras long ago: the asuras were the elder brothers, and the gods the younger." That war ended in a flood of blood: "the war lasted thirty-two thousand years; making the earth a single ocean, flooded with blood, the gods killed the Daityas." Daityas is another name for the asuras. And Vyāsa tells the king, "you have walked the road the gods walked before".

Mahābhārata 12.34.13-15, 21, our rendering

The older Upaniṣads, the philosophical teachings of the first millennium BC, say it before the epic. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, in Patrick Olivelle's translation of 1998, has both peoples as children of the creator Prajāpati: "the gods were the younger of his offspring, while the demons were the older; and they were competing for these worlds." Śaṅkara, the great commentator of the eighth century AD, read the elder and younger as a headcount: "the gods were few, the asuras more numerous".

Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.3.1, tr. Olivelle 1998 · Śaṅkara on 1.3.1, our rendering

The word asura was once a title of honour. In the Rigveda, the oldest of the Indian hymn books, a hymn to Varuṇa, the god of the sky's law, says "the all-knowing asura propped up the sky", and a refrain sings that "great is the one asura-hood of the gods". The word meant lord. The younger gods took the lordship, and the name of lord was left to the losers. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, a priests' manual of the first millennium BC, says the gods even took their speech from them, and makes it a rule of language: "Hence let no Brahman speak barbarous language, since such is the speech of the Asuras." The Mahābhārata says what became of the beaten elders, using its other names for both sides, Daityas for the asuras and Ādityas for the gods: "for the Daityas, many times beaten in battle by the Ādityas and fallen from sovereignty, were born here on earth." They were born again as kings of men, and the weight of those kings is the subject of a later chapter, The Burden.

Rigveda 8.42.1, 3.55, our rendering · Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.2.1.24, tr. Eggeling (SBE) · Mahābhārata 1.58.26, our rendering

The deposed asura king keeps a promise of return. In the Mahābhārata Indra, king of the gods, sees Śrī, royal glory and good fortune in a woman's form, leave the body of the asura king Bali. She tells Indra, calling him by his other name Śakra, that no god decides her movements: "Time, Śakra, moves me in its turns; do not despise him". Bali, beaten, promises he will rise again "when the sun burns the worlds standing in one place", that is, at the end of an age, when the sun stands still over the world and burns it.

Mahābhārata 12.218.10, 32, our rendering

Iran has the same two words with good and evil swapped. In India the asuras are the beaten elders and the devas are the gods; in Iran the ahura (the Iranian form of asura) is the Lord, and the daevas (the Iranian form of deva) are demons. The Wise Lord of Iran is Ahura Mazda, and the Vendidad, the Zoroastrian law against the demons, names some of the gods of India among the daevas: "I drive away Indra, I drive away Sauru, I drive away the daeva Naunghaithya". The two words have been matched by language scholars for two centuries, but the records in this chapter do not set the two religions side by side, and the setting is ours ◇. A modern pair of writers made a larger match. Giorgio de Santillana and Hertha von Dechend, in Hamlet's Mill of 1969, a study of star-lore in myth, put the Greek Titans and the Indian asuras together as the powers of an earlier order of the world, and noted that in both tellings the elders are the ones said to have done wrong first.

Vendidad 10.9, tr. Darmesteter 1880 · Hamlet's Mill, p. 153

Before Adam

Islam's histories put a people on the earth before mankind. Al-Ṭabarī, the historian of Baghdad who died in 923, collected what the first Muslims said of it, from the companion of the Prophet Ibn ʿAbbās: "The first to dwell on earth were the jinn. They caused corruption on it and shed blood and killed each other." The jinn are the people the Qurʾān says God made from smokeless fire. God sent Iblis, who in Islam is the devil, the one who would later refuse to bow to Adam, against them "with an army of angels", and they were driven to the islands of the ocean and the mountainsides. Iblis himself, before his fall, had another name, ʿAzāzīl, and one of al-Ṭabarī's reports says that "He belonged to a tribal group called jinn."

al-Ṭabarī, History I:79-84, tr. Rosenthal 1989

The elder angel's refusal to bow to the younger creature is told in the Life of Adam and Eve, a book of the first centuries AD that stands outside the Bible and survives in Latin. When God made Adam and the archangel Michael called all the angels to worship him, the devil answered: "I will not worship an inferior and younger being (than I). I am his senior in the Creation, before he was made was I already made. It is his duty to worship me." In this telling the elder is cast down because he would not bow to the younger, the reverse of the Greek and Hittite tellings, where the younger casts down the elder. Milton gave the rebel angels the elder's claim in the boldest words of all: "We know no time when we were not as now; / Know none before us, self-begot, self-raised / By our own quickening power". In the poem the words are Satan's, the loyal angel Abdiel answers them to his face, and the rebels lose the war in heaven that follows.

Life of Adam and Eve 14.3, tr. Wells (Charles 1913) · Paradise Lost 5.859-861, Milton 1667

The Yezidis, a people of northern Iraq who speak Kurdish, keep a first-created angel who was never thrown down. In the English of Isya Joseph, 1919, their Black Book says: "On the first day, Sunday, God created Melek Anzazîl, and he is Ṭâ'ûs-Melek, the chief of all." Ṭâ'ûs-Melek is the Peacock Angel. The Yezidi Anzazîl and the ʿAzāzīl that Islam gives as Iblis's first name are nearly one name; neither book sets the two side by side, and pointing out the likeness is ours ◇. The Yezidi book answers the peoples who call him a fallen angel: "But the foreign sects, ignorant of this fact, say that our god came from heaven, despised and driven out by the great God. For this reason they blaspheme him. In this they have erred." That is the Yezidis' claim against the others. For Jews, Christians and Muslims the first angel who would not bow is the adversary; for the Yezidis he was never driven out. The chapter gives both as their peoples tell them.

Mashaf Reš and al-Jilwah, tr. Joseph 1919

Other islands and valleys had first holders too. The Dīpavaṃsa, the chronicle of the island of Sri Lanka written in the fourth century AD, says that when the Buddha came "In the present time Yakkhas, Bhutas and Rakkhasas (inhabit) Lankadipa", spirits and demons who held the island before men. Kalhaṇa's chronicle of Kashmir, written in 1148 and put into English by Aurel Stein in 1900, says the valley "is protected by Nila, the lord of all Nagas", and Stein explains the Nāgas as "the tutelary deities which are supposed to reside in the springs and lakes of the Valley", still honoured in his day by Hindus and Muslims alike.

Dīpavaṃsa I, tr. Oldenberg 1879 · Rājataraṅgiṇī I.28, tr. Stein 1900

The false sun and the giants of the first sun

The Maya of Guatemala remember an elder who claimed the sky before the true sun rose. The Popol Vuh, the Book of the Council of the Kʼicheʼ Maya, was written down in the Latin alphabet in the 1550s and survives in a copy made by the Dominican friar Francisco Ximénez about 1701. Before the dawn of the present world, when the face of the earth was only a little lit, a great bird called Seven Macaw puffed himself up over the people who had just been made, and said: "I am their sun. I am also their light. And I am also their moon." The book gives its verdict at once: "Now Seven Macaw was not truly the sun". The Hero Twins, two young brothers whose victories over the old powers make the world ready for the true dawn, shot him down and stripped him of his jewels, and his two sons, who tore down mountains and raised them up, were brought down after him; one of them was made into stone under a mountain. When the true sun rose at last, the book says, it showed itself whole only at that first rising, and the sun men see now is its reflection: "What is left is but a mirror. What appears now is not the true sun, according to their account."

Popol Vuh, pp. 77-78, 214, tr. Christenson 2007

In Mexico the Nahua, the people of the Aztecs, said the world had already passed through earlier suns, and that the first one was a sun of giants. The History of the Mexicans by their Paintings, written in Spanish about 1535 in the circle of the friar Andrés de Olmos from the Mexicans' painted books, says that in that first age all the gods raised up the giants, men so great and strong that they pulled up trees with their hands. They ate acorns and nothing else, and they lasted as long as that sun lasted, thirteen times fifty-two years, which is 676 years. The Annals of Cuauhtitlan, a Nahuatl chronicle, keep the giants' greeting, do not fall, because whoever of them fell, fell for ever.

Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas, cap. 3 · Anales de Cuauhtitlan, p. 5, tr. Velázquez

Africa has the elder who lost the making of the world. The Yoruba of Nigeria told the German traveller Leo Frobenius, about 1910, that the high god Olorun sent the elder god Orisha down with earth to make the land upon the sea. "On the way he found some palm-wine. He began to drink and got drunken. Then he slumbered." Odudua, the younger, took the earth and made the land instead, and the two fought, then made peace: "They both went underground and were never seen again". Among the Dogon of Mali the elder Ogotemmêli taught the French ethnographer Marcel Griaule in October 1946 that the first birth of the creator Amma went wrong: where twins were intended, a single being came out, the jackal, a sign of God's difficulties. The first-born of creation came alone and brought disorder with him, so he is the elder who lost his place; yet in Griaule's book he is the one who was given speech, and through him the diviners read God's designs, which makes him also an elder who keeps a knowledge.

Frobenius, The Voice of Africa I, p. 283, 1913 · Griaule, Dieu d'eau, p. 24

The long reigns, and the gears

Sumer's King List, a list of cities and their kings copied in the Old Babylonian period (the best copy, the Weld-Blundell prism, is from about 1800 BC), gives the first kings reigns that no man lives. "In Eridug, Alulim became king; he ruled for 28800 years. Alaljar ruled for 36000 years." Eridug is Eridu in the far south of Iraq. Four more cities follow, and the list closes the age in one line: "In 5 cities 8 kings; they ruled for 241200 years. Then the flood swept over." After the flood the kingship comes down again and the reigns begin to shorten.

Sumerian King List 1-39, ETCSL

Berossus counts ten kings before the flood where the King List has eight. In the summary of Alexander Polyhistor, a Greek compiler of the first century BC, their reigns came to "an hundred and twenty sari, or four hundred and thirty-two thousand years; reaching to the time of the Deluge." A sar, Abydenus explains, is a unit of years: "a sarus is esteemed to be three thousand six hundred years". The last of the ten kings is Xisuthrus, the flood hero, the Sumerian Ziusudra. Genesis has its own line of ten long-lived fathers from Adam to Noah before the flood, the longest-lived of them Methuselah at 969 years; setting Berossus's ten kings beside Genesis's ten fathers is ours ◇.

Polyhistor and Abydenus, in Cory 1832

Egypt counted gods as its first kings. The Turin Royal Canon, a list of kings written on the back of a tax papyrus under Ramesses II about 1245 BC, has "Total: 9 kings from Re to Haroeris", the gods from Ra the sun to Horus the Elder, then the "reign of the spirits and the Followers of Horus 13,420 (+x)", before Menes, the first human king. Manetho, an Egyptian priest who wrote his country's history in Greek in the third century BC, gave the gods' reigns in thousands of years too.

Turin Royal Canon, cols. 1-3, tr. Gardiner; Ryholt

Later readers tried to bring these numbers down to human size by changing the unit, and their attempts belong to the story as much as the reigns. Eusebius, receiving Manetho's divine ages, explained them by a change in the word for year: "what we now call a month the Egyptians formerly styled a year." Panodorus, a monk of Alexandria about AD 400, read Berossus's 432,000 years as days and got about 1,183 years. Two modern popular writers have done the same with their own units. Jason Breshears, who writes on the chronology of the ages, reads each year of the King List as a day of a 360-day year, so that its 241,200 years become 670 years. Zecharia Sitchin, in The 12th Planet of 1976, took the sar of 3,600 years for the orbit of an unknown planet around the sun. These are their readings; the old lists themselves give the years as they stand.

Eusebius on Manetho, tr. Waddell (Loeb) · Panodorus in Syncellus (Concordance I.7) · Breshears (ERRATA A2) · Sitchin, The 12th Planet, 1976, pp. 247-248

Egypt's exception

Egypt is the one archive in this chapter where the eldest king is not thrown down by his son. In the Book of the Heavenly Cow, painted in the tombs of the New Kingdom pharaohs (the version cited here is Seti I's, of the thirteenth century BC), the sun god Ra once ruled gods and men together: "and mankind and gods were still united." Then he grew old, "his bones of silver, his flesh of gold, his hair of real lapis lazuli." Men plotted against him, a "conspiracy while the king is old", and Ra called the elders older than himself, "the fathers and mothers who were with me when I was in the primordial water, and also my god Nun". Nun is the water before the world. Ra punished mankind, and then he withdrew: "My body is weak for the first time." He went up to heaven on the back of the cow of the sky and left the earth to others.

Book of the Heavenly Cow (Seti I), cols. 1-5 and the withdrawal, translator not named

Herodotus, the Greek historian who visited Egypt about 450 BC, heard the same from the Egyptian priests: "gods were the rulers in Egypt, not being together with men", and in the 11,340 years of the human kings since, "no god appeared in human form". In Egypt the gods withdrew, and nobody threw them down.

Herodotus 2.142-144, our rendering

Egypt does have a thrown-down elder, and he is a brother. Osiris was "first of his brothers, eldest of the Ennead", the Nine Gods of Heliopolis, in a hymn on a stela now in the Louvre, and the Pyramid Texts, the spells carved in the pyramids from about 2350 BC, tell that "they found Osiris after his brother Seth had laid him low in Nedit" and that "that Great One is fallen on his side; he who is in Nedit is thrown down". He was raised again, and he reigns over the dead. The temple of Dendera, rebuilt under the Ptolemies and the Romans, kept a list of the god's relics laid in the provinces of Egypt, "in total, fourteen objects which are divine relics": the eldest brother's body divided across the land, as Tiamat's and Ymir's were made into it.

Louvre stela C 286 · Pyramid Texts 442, 532 · Dendera, Khoiak inscription, tr. Cauville

Who was first among peoples

The quarrel over who came first was fought among peoples as well as gods, and on the same terms: to be eldest was to hold a title the later ones had to answer to. Herodotus tells how Psammetichus, king of Egypt in the seventh century BC, tried to settle it: "Now before Psammetichus became king of Egypt, the Egyptians believed that they were the oldest people on earth." He had two newborn children raised where they would hear no speech, and after two years "both children ran to him stretching out their hands and calling 'Bekos!' as he opened the door and entered." The king's men "found it to be a Phrygian word, signifying bread. Reasoning from this, the Egyptians acknowledged that the Phrygians were older than they." The Phrygians lived in the uplands of western Anatolia.

Herodotus 2.2, tr. Godley 1920

Other peoples made their own claims. The Phrygians had a king from before the flood: Zenobius, a Greek collector of proverbs in the second century AD, explains a saying about weeping for him, "For Nannakos was king of the Phrygians, as Hermogenes says in his Phrygian History, before the times of Deucalion". Deucalion is the Greek flood hero. Diodorus gave the first place to the Ethiopians, by which the Greeks meant the peoples south of Egypt, in Kush, the Nile country of the modern Sudan: "the Ethiopians, as historians relate, were the first of all men". Kush said it of itself. On a stela that the pharaoh Thutmose III set up at the holy mountain of Gebel Barkal in Kush about 1430 BC, in the forty-seventh year of his reign, the mountain carries a name older than knowledge: "people of the southern land who are at the Pure Mountain, which was called 'Thrones of the Two Lands' among men when it was not yet known". The Greeks also heard the Persian claim: Aristotle, says Diogenes Laertius, declared "that the Magi are more ancient than the Egyptians".

Zenobius 6.10, our rendering · Diodorus 3.2.1, tr. Oldfather · Gebel Barkal stela of Thutmose III, 33-34, our rendering · Diogenes Laertius 1.8, tr. Hicks (Loeb)

What is kept

The elder thrown down is kept, and the records keep him in the four ways named at the start. The first is sleep. Plutarch heard from a traveller that "Cronus is confined by Zeus" on an island beyond Britain, in the western ocean, asleep, "for his sleep has been devised as a bondage for him". Spirits attend him there, and he dreams what Zeus intends. Milton, writing of the Greek gods' wanderings, sent the old king the same way, "or who with Saturn old / Fled over Adria to th' Hesperian fields, / And o'er the Celtic roamed the utmost Isles." Plutarch's island beyond Britain and Milton's utmost Isles over the Celtic land lie in the same direction, and putting them together is ours ◇.

Plutarch, On the Face in the Moon 941a, tr. Cherniss; On the Obsolescence of Oracles 420a, tr. Babbitt · Paradise Lost 1.519-521, Milton 1667

The second is the oath. The Hittite kings swore by the olden gods, and the Greek gods did the same. In the Iliad Hera, the queen of the gods, swears an oath and "invoked by name all the gods below Tartarus, that are called Titans". The young order, having bound the old, calls the old to witness its word.

Iliad 14.278-279, tr. Murray 1925

The third is the ground. Ymir is the earth, Tiamat the sky and the land, Asag the stone of the mountains, Ouranos the springs where his blood ran, and the Titans and Kumarbi live under the earth. In Japan the god who had ruled the land before the heavenly line, Ōkuninushi, the Great Land Master of Izumo, gives it up when the gods of heaven send to claim it. In the Nihon shoki, the Chronicles of Japan finished in 720, the message from heaven divides the world: what is visible he has ruled, and the emperor's line will rule it now, while he will rule the divine affairs. He answers "I will withdraw and govern the hidden affairs (幽事)", and he "hides for ever". The elder is moved out of sight and keeps the hidden half of the world.

Nihon shoki, Age of the Gods, Chinese as Unit 7 gives it (frag. 32), our rendering

The fourth is the knowledge the elder still holds. Under the root of the world-tree that turns toward the Rime-Giants is "Mímir's Well, wherein wisdom and understanding are stored", and Odin, the chief of the younger gods, "got it not until he had laid his eye in pledge." In the Kalevala the sage Väinämöinen must go down into the body of the giant Antero Vipunen, who lies under the earth with trees growing on him, to get the words he lacks, and Vipunen opens to him "Magic songs of the Creation, / From the very earliest ages,". In the Irish story of the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the Fomoire, the people who held Ireland before the gods, are led by Balor, whose one eye kills whatever it looks at and is so heavy that it must be lifted for him; he calls out, in the Irish, lift my eyelid, lad. His grandson Lug kills him, and at the end of the war the Fomoire are driven back to the sea. The French scholar Henri d'Arbois de Jubainville, in 1884, took Balor for Kronos.

Gylfaginning 15, tr. Brodeur 1916 · Kalevala 17.527-532, tr. Kirby 1907 · Cath Maige Tuired 621, 694-695, ed. Gray 1982

The four ways outlived the old poems, and the last overthrow in this chapter shows the first of them again, an elder who comes back on certain days. In 988 Vladimir, prince of Kiev, was baptized, and the Russian Primary Chronicle tells what he did with the city's chief god: "He thus ordered that Perun should be bound to a horse's tail and dragged along Borichev to the river." For the chronicler, a Christian monk of Kiev, this was the true God putting down an idol, and he says so in his own way: "Yesterday he was honored of men, but today held in derision." The Novgorod chronicle has a man of that city push the floating idol away from the bank with the words "You, little Perun," "have drunk and eaten your fill, and now float away". Yet Perun was kept too. Sigismund von Herberstein, an envoy of the Habsburg emperor to Muscovy in 1517 and 1526, heard at Novgorod that a voice had come from the river and a rope was thrown onto the bridge, and that "Even now it happens from time to time on certain days of the year, that this voice of Perun may be heard". On those days Novgorod still heard the god it had thrown into the river.

Primary Chronicle s.a. 988, tr. Cross 1930 · Novgorod First Chronicle s.a. 989, our rendering · Herberstein, tr. Major 1852

So the records of many peoples agree on the first beat of the story. Before the present gods there were elders: older kings of heaven in Hatti, Greece and Phoenicia, older brothers in India, an older people on the earth in Islam's histories, and a false sun before the true one among the Maya. The younger threw them down and took the rule, and in nearly every one of these records the elders are still there, asleep in the west or sworn by at the oath, lying under the ground or made into it, keeping a well or the hidden half of the world. In these records the present order stands on them. Genesis stands apart, with a God who has no elder, and Egypt's gods withdrew rather than fell.

What came after them came down from above. Sumer's King List, which closed the age of the long reigns with the flood, opens with kingship coming down from heaven, and the records of many peoples say that in the first age the way between heaven and earth stood open. That way, and what came down it, is the next chapter.
