II. Going Down

Before this chapter comes The Elders, which told how the first holders of heaven were thrown down and kept. This chapter follows what came down after them, and by what road. An archive, in this book, is the body of old writings of one people or one tradition. Where we, and no old source, link two archives, the text says the link is ours and marks it ◇; where an old author made the link himself, it is said as his. The line under each paragraph that begins with ~ names the sources; where it says our rendering, the English is this book's own. Under each section, Told by names the archives that told it, with where and roughly when they were written, and the ledger's count where it has one. The ledger is this book's own tally of the passages it has gathered from the archives, each passage filed under the thing it tells, so that a count such as 110 passages across 19 archives means that many passages, from the writings of that many peoples, tell that one thing; the Names table is the volume's list of each figure's and each place's names in every language the records give.

Chapter I ended with the elders thrown down and kept, and with Sumer's King List opening on a kingship that came down from heaven. The records of many peoples say that in the first age the way between heaven and earth stood open, and this book calls that open way the door. In these records the way is a thing. It is a cord a king comes down, a tree pulled up to leave a hole in the floor of the sky, a ladder with a count of steps, a chain whose links are hands, and the records show it used in both directions: Homer's gods, in the Greek poem of the war at Troy, are back on Olympus by the twelfth day, and Tibet's first kings, in that country's own histories, go back up the cord when they die. What came down it was an office, a post with a title, before it was a king, a crown that lay in heaven waiting for a head. After the office came heaven's own people, sent down or asking to be sent, and a few thrown out by the foot; the ones who went down grew heavy where they landed, took the earth in portions and swore on them, and left behind them names that the next holder could wear. The way back is the chapter's question, and the close will show what the records say of it, each record speaking for its own people and for no other's: that in record after record the way back was lost at the moment the one who went down wanted something that was below, long before anyone shut it. The chapter watches, record by record, for that moment.

A cord, a ladder, a bean-stalk

Tibet's first seven kings have no tombs, because each of them went back up the way he had come down. The kings who ruled the high plateau from the seventh century AD traced their line to a first king, Nyatri Tsenpo, who came down from the sky onto a mountain, and the later histories, written by Buddhist scholars such as Bu-ston in the fourteenth century, say that the priests of Bon, Tibet's older religion, knew him for a god because he had come by the dmu-cord and the dmu-ladder, the sky-cord and the sky-ladder, and that when they asked him who he was he answered Tsenpo, which became the title of every king after him. The Old Tibetan Chronicle, a scroll of the ninth century found in the cave library of Dunhuang in western China, uses older words for the same two things, a head-cord and a "head-ladder of nine steps", which the later histories gloss as the dmu-cord and the dmu-ladder. A rock inscription at Kongpo in south-eastern Tibet, cut about AD 800, says the first king "came to Lha-ri Gyang-do to rule over the land of men for seven generations". The Sa-skya king-list counts them, Nyatri, Dengtri, Mutri, Pritri, Daktri, Triye and Drigum, and calls the seven together the Seven Thrones of Heaven. W. W. Rockhill, an American who read the Tibetan histories in 1884, calls the first king and his six successors the seven celestial Khri, khri being the Tibetan word for throne, and gives the reason there are no tombs: when they died, their bodies were carried off to heaven. Seven came down by the cord and went up by it without leaving a body; the Sa-skya list counts Drigum as the seventh of its Seven Thrones, and his cord is cut, which is told in the chapter The Cut.

Bu-ston, the Sa-skya king-list and Rockhill 1884, told in our words · the Old Tibetan Chronicle, PT 1287, our rendering · Kongpo inscription, tr. Richardson 1985, p. 66

The Iroquois put a tree where Tibet puts a cord. The Haudenosaunee, which is what the Iroquois call themselves, of what is now New York State and southern Ontario place a world above the sky with a tree at its centre, and J. N. B. Hewitt, an ethnologist of Tuscarora descent, wrote down three tellings in the tellers' own languages with English beside them, from a Seneca, a Mohawk and an Onondaga, and printed them in 1903. The Mohawk tree "was constantly adorned with blossoms that gave light to the people dwelling there; for these flowers were white", so that "they were the light orb [sun] of the people dwelling there". The tree was pulled up and left a hole through the floor of the sky, and the woman of the sky world was pushed through it by her husband. Two tellers describe the same push. The Seneca: "Just as soon as she bent her neck, he seized her leg and pushed her body down thither." The Mohawk: "he placed his fingers against the nape of her neck and pushed her, and she fell into the hole". Then the sky people closed the way behind her: "They immediately reset the tree, so that it [stood as before]". She fell toward a world of water, and the earth was made for her on the back of a turtle.

Iroquoian Cosmology, Mohawk pp. 282-285, Seneca p. 223, ed. and tr. Hewitt 1903

In Latvia the way up is a bean-stalk and a singer climbs it. The dainas, the four-line folk songs of the Latvians that Krišjānis Barons printed between 1894 and 1915, keep a household in the sky, the god Dievs with his sons and the Sun with her daughters, and one song goes up to look at them: "Es uzkāpu debesīs / Pa pupiņas zariņiem; / Es redzēju Dieva dēlus / Kumeliņus seglojot." In our English, I climbed up to heaven by the little branches of the bean; I saw the sons of God saddling their horses. The singer asks one of them where he is riding, and he answers "Jāšu sievas lūkoties, / Saules meitas precībās.", I shall ride to look for a wife, to court the Sun's daughter. Dieva dēli, the sons of God, is what the songs call the riders, and the same two words come back in this chapter from the Latvian Bible.

Latvian daina LD 34046 (Barons), English ours

Among the Dogon of Mali the way down was a chain, and the chain was the family. The Dogon elder Ogotemmêli taught the French ethnographer Marcel Griaule in 1946 that the first ancestors came down from heaven in an ark, and Griaule and Germaine Dieterlen's later book, Le Renard pâle of 1965, says what the ark hung from: "L'arche était suspendue à une chaîne de cuivre ou de fer dont les anneaux symbolisaient les ancêtres se tenant par la main", the ark hung from a chain of copper or iron whose links stood for the ancestors holding hands. It came to ground where the bow of the sky met the earth: "Survint l'impact final qui se produisit au bout de l'arc-en-ciel, là où il touchait la terre", then came the last shock, at the end of the rainbow, where it touched the earth. The way down was made of the ancestors themselves.

Griaule and Dieterlen, Le Renard pâle, 1965, via Bonnet-Bidaud, English ours · Griaule, Dieu d'eau, 1948, p. 49

Further south the way down was an errand with a parcel. The Yoruba of Nigeria told Leo Frobenius, a German traveller who gathered their stories between 1910 and 1912, that the high god Olorun called the elder of two gods, Orisha, and loaded him: "He gave him some earth. He gave him a hen with five fingers", and sent him off with the words "'Climb down' (or, Go down to earth), 'and make the earth upon Okun.'" Okun is the sea. Orisha found palm-wine on the way, drank, and slept, and the younger god made the land instead; that quarrel is Chapter I's. What this chapter keeps is the verb. Climb down: the way between the high god's house and the sea was something a god went down hand over hand.

Frobenius, The Voice of Africa I, p. 283, 1913

China remembers the way as a thing used only by the fit. The Guoyu, the Discourses of the States, a collection of speeches from the kingdoms of the Zhou age put together about the fourth century BC, has Guan Shefu, a minister of the southern kingdom of Chu, explain to his king how things stood at first: "Anciently the people and the spirits did not mix. Those among the people whose essence was undivided, who could be reverent, centred and upright … on such the bright spirits descended; in a man he was called xi, in a woman wu." The spirits came down, and they came down only on the undivided. Everywhere else in this section the way is a thing, a cord or a tree; in the Guoyu alone it is a person, the man or the woman the spirits came down on. How the people and the spirits came to be mixed, and how the way was shut on that account, is The Cut's to tell.

Guoyu 18.1 (Chu yu), our rendering

The Hebrew Bible has one man see the way itself. In Genesis, Jacob, the grandson of Abraham, lies down for the night on his way out of the land with a stone for his pillow, and dreams of a ladder set on the earth with its top reaching heaven, and the angels of God going up and down on it. Genesis gives the two directions in that order, up before down, and gives them to the same travellers.

Genesis 28:12, in our words

Babylon set a keeper at the crossing. Enūma eliš, the creation poem Chapter I opened with, ends by giving Marduk, the god of Babylon, fifty names, and one of them is a star in the middle of the sky, Neberu, whose name means Crossing; he holds the crossing place of heaven and earth, and the gods above and below must wait for him before they cross.

Enūma eliš VII 124-125, tr. Foster (eBL), in our words

Homer's gods still used it for dinner. In the first book of the Iliad, the Greek poem of the war at Troy sung in the eighth or seventh century BC, the sea goddess Thetis cannot take the plea of her son Achilles, the best of the Greek fighters, to Zeus at once, "for Zeus went yesterday to Oceanus, to the blameless Ethiopians for a feast, and all the gods followed with him; but on the twelfth day he will come back again to Olympus". The Odyssey, the poem of the long way home, sends Poseidon to the same people, "the Ethiopians who dwell sundered in twain, the farthermost of men, some where Hyperion sets and some where he rises, there to receive a hecatomb of bulls and rams". Oceanus is the river the Greeks thought ran round the rim of the earth, Hyperion is the sun, and a hecatomb is a sacrifice of a hundred cattle. The Roman poet Ovid, in the Metamorphoses of about AD 8, has Jupiter make the same trip downward with a purpose: "I descended from high Olympus, and as a god disguised in human form travelled up and down the land." He went down to see for himself what report had told him, and came back up to say it was true.

Iliad 1.423-425, tr. Murray 1924 · Odyssey 1.22-25, tr. Murray 1919 · Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.212-213, tr. Miller 1916

A cord, a tree, a bean-stalk, a chain of hands, a ladder with angels on it, a road to the rim and back by the twelfth day: in these records the way is a thing with two ends, and every traveller on it, so far, has used both.

The crown before the king

Before there was a king there was already a crown, and it lay in heaven. The Babylonian poem of Etana, copied in the first half of the second millennium BC, opens on an earth with people and no ruler: "Among all the teeming peoples they had established no king", and no headdress or crown had yet been made, nor any sceptre set with lapis lazuli. The things themselves were waiting above, in front of the sky god: "Scepter, crown, headdress, and staff / Were set before Anu in heaven." When the people had no right counsel, "(Then) did [kin]gship come down from heaven." The man it came down to was Etana, a shepherd of Kish, who later rode an eagle up to heaven to look for the plant of birth, and so is the first in this chapter to make the trip in both directions.

Etana OB, tr. Foster (eBL)

Sumer's King List says the same as flatly as a calendar lists its dates. The list of the cities and kings of Sumer in southern Iraq, copied early in the second millennium BC, begins "After the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridug.", Eridu in the far south, and when the flood has passed it begins again in the same words, "After the flood had swept over", and the kingship descends a second time, to Kish in the north of Babylonia, where the list names "Etana, the shepherd, who ascended to heaven". A Sumerian telling of the flood, on a tablet from Nippur of about 1600 BC, puts the regalia first and the cities after: "after the exalted crown and throne of kingship had descended from heaven", the rites were perfected, the bricks of the first cities were laid in holy places, and each city was given to a god. Kingship is the thing that comes down in this list, and the kings are where it lands.

Sumerian King List 1, 40-42, 64-67, ETCSL · the Sumerian Flood story, Segment B 6-18, ETCSL

On the steppe the office fell out of the sky in gold. Herodotus, the Greek historian of the fifth century BC, heard from the Scythians, the horse people north of the Black Sea, that in the days of their first three brothers golden things fell from heaven, a plough with a yoke, a battle-axe and a cup for pouring offerings; the gold burned when the two elder brothers came near it, and went out for the youngest, Kolaxais, who carried it home, and his brothers gave over to him "the whole kingship". The signs chose their holder by fire. Whoever fell asleep in the open with the gold at the Scythians' yearly festival did not live out the year. In Peru the first Inca brothers, once out of the cave they came from, sent one of them back into it for their sign of office, and Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, a Spaniard who wrote the history of the Incas for the viceroy of Peru in 1572, names it: "the napa, which is our principal ensign of sovereignty."

Herodotus 4.5-7, our rendering · Sarmiento, tr. Markham

Korea's office came down in a hand that had already reached for it. The Samguk yusa, the Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms, which the Buddhist monk Iryeon compiled about 1280, quotes an Old Record on Hwanin, the king of heaven (Iryeon adds in a bracket that this is Śakra, the Buddhist name of Indra, king of the gods), and his son by a secondary wife, Hwanung, "who again and again set his mind on the world below and coveted the human world". The father looked where the son was looking: "The father knew his son's mind; looking down on the Three Dangers and Taebaek, he saw they could be of broad benefit to mankind. So he gave him three heavenly seals and sent him to go and govern it." The Three Dangers and Taebaek are mountains. Hwanung "led a company of three thousand and came down to the top of Mount Taebaek" and set up his rule "under the tree of the divine altar; they called it the City of God". He did not come down alone or empty-handed: "He took with him the Wind Master, the Rain Master and the Cloud Master", and he had charge of grain and life, sickness and punishment, good and evil, "in all, of some three hundred and sixty human matters." Homer Hulbert, an American missionary who wrote Korea's history in English in 1905, called the son's trouble celestial ennui, which is to say boredom with heaven. His son Dangun was the first king of Korea.

Samguk yusa 1, Gojoseon, quoting the Old Record, our rendering · Hulbert 1905

Iryeon says the like of every founder in the peninsula and refuses to be surprised by it. Korea in the first centuries AD was three kingdoms, Goguryeo in the north and Baekje and Silla in the south, which is what the title of his book means; before them, on the plain to the north, lay Buyeo. The Heavenly Emperor "came down to the city of Heulseunggol … riding a chariot of five dragons; he founded a capital and called himself king, named the state Northern Buyeo, and called himself Hae Mosu." Silla's founder was named Hyeokgeose, a word of the local tongue, the monk says, also written Bulgunae, meaning "light governs the world". The Samguk sagi, the older official history of 1145, has Jumong, founder of Goguryeo, run from his pursuers to a river and call out his descent: "I am the son of the Heavenly Emperor and the grandson of the River Earl." The river answered: "At that, fish and turtles rose and made a bridge." Having set all this down, Iryeon turns to the reader: "So then, the founders of the Three Kingdoms all arose out of divine wonders. What is there to marvel at?"

Samguk yusa 1, our rendering · Samguk sagi 13, our rendering

The Mongols kept the light that came in at the roof. The Secret History of the Mongols, the chronicle of Chinggis Khan's house written down in the thirteenth century, has the widow Alan Qoa answer her two elder sons, who had been counting months since their father's death and could not account for three brothers: "Every night a yellowish-white man came in by the bright place of the smoke-hole and the lintel of the door and rubbed my belly. When his light had passed into my belly, he went out along the light of the sun and moon, crawling out like a yellow dog." She tells them what that makes the three: "they are clearly the sons of Heaven" and "are not to be compared with ordinary men. Later, when they become emperors and kings, then [men] will know." The light came in by the smoke-hole and went out the same way, and the khans came of the youngest of the three.

Secret History of the Mongols §21 (Yuanchao bishi), our rendering

An older steppe people had asked Heaven to come down for a bride, and Heaven came. The Weishu, the history of the Northern Wei dynasty of north China finished in 554, has a steppe king with two daughters so fair that he would give them to no man: "I will give them to Heaven." He set them on a high terrace in an empty place and said "I ask Heaven to come and receive them itself." When their mother wanted them home he refused: "No. It is only that the time has not yet run through." An old wolf came and kept the terrace, and the younger daughter read the sign, "Perhaps it is a divine thing, and Heaven has made it so." Her sister answered "This is a beast; would that not shame our father and mother?" The younger went down to the wolf, and the Gaoche, a people of the steppe, count their line from her; "That is why these people like to draw out their voices in long songs that also sound like a wolf's howl."

Weishu 103, our rendering

In the North the god came down on foot and handed the first nobleman his rank. The Rígsþula, the Lay of Rig, a Norse poem kept only on the last damaged leaf of a fourteenth-century Icelandic manuscript, says in its prose opening that Heimdall, the watchman of the gods, walked the earth under the name Rig and stayed in three houses; the sons born after his visits founded the ranks of men, from thrall to earl. To the earl he came back: "Straight from the grove came striding Rig, / Rig came striding, and runes he taught him; / By his name he called him, as son he claimed him, / And bade him hold his heritage wide, / His heritage wide, the ancient homes." The Völuspá, the Prophecy of the Seeress, the Norse poem of the world's beginning and end, opens by calling all mankind his: "Hearing I ask from the holy races, / From Heimdall's sons, both high and low". Tacitus, the Roman historian, wrote in AD 98 that the Germans sang of a god and his son as the fathers of the race, and that "Some, however, assuming the licence of antiquity, affirm that there were more descendants of the god, from whom more appellations were derived; as those of the Marsi, Gambrivii, Suevi, and Vandali; and that these are the genuine and original names". The Latin for the claim is two words, "pluris deo ortos", more sprung from the god: the tribes carried the names of the god's sons.

Rígsþula 36, tr. Bellows 1923 · Völuspá 1, tr. Bellows 1923 · Tacitus, Germania 2, Oxford translation

Egypt's Greek writers said the whole land was an office come down. The Asclepius, a Hermetic dialogue, one of the Greek writings of the first centuries AD that Egyptians set under the name of Hermes Trismegistus, Hermes Thrice-Greatest, their Greek name for the god Thoth, has Hermes teach a pupil named Asclepius, and tells him that "Egypt is the image of the Heaven; or, what is truer still, the transference, or the descent, of all that are in governance or exercise in Heaven". Whatever governs above, in this book, has come down and governs in Egypt.

Asclepius 24, tr. Mead 1906

One great archive tells it the other way round, with its humour intact. In the Aggañña Sutta, the Buddha's discourse on what is first known, in the Long Discourses of the Pali canon, the Buddhist scriptures in the Pali language, written down in Sri Lanka in the first century BC, the first beings of a new world come down from a higher heaven, lose their light by eating (the section The finger in the savoury earth tells it), and in time take to stealing from one another, so they meet and reason it out: "what if we were to select a certain being, who should be wrathful when indignation is right, who should censure that which should rightly be censured and should banish him who deserves to be banished ? But we will give him in return a proportion of the rice." They go to the one among them who is "the handsomest, the best favoured, the most attractive, the most capable", and he agrees, and they pay him. The Buddha explains the office to Vāseṭṭha, the young man of the Brahmin priestly caste he is talking to, by its name: "Chosen by the whole people, Vāseṭṭha, is what is meant by Mahā Sammata ; so Mahā Sammata (the Great Elect) was the first standing phrase to arise". No seals, no gold, no chariot of five dragons, no light at the smoke-hole: "Their origin was from among those very beings, and no others ; like unto themselves, not unlike".

Aggañña Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 27.20-21, tr. Rhys Davids 1921

In every archive here but one the office was in heaven before it had a holder, and in Korea it came down to a hand that was already out, the hand of a son who had coveted the human world before his father looked down to see whether it would do. Among the Buddhists the holders found the office, and paid for it in rice.

Eight years and no report

Japan sent two envoys down before it sent a king, and neither came back. Japan's two oldest books, the Nihongi, the Chronicles of Japan finished in 720 and put into English by W. G. Aston in 1896, and the Kojiki of 712, in Basil Hall Chamberlain's English of 1882, tell the going down as one continuous story, and it is the fullest telling of why anyone went. Heaven looked down and did not like what it saw. "But in that Land there were numerous Deities which shone with a lustre like that of fireflies, and evil Deities which buzzed like flies. There were also trees and herbs all of which could speak." In the Kojiki a son of the sun goddess stands on the Floating Bridge of Heaven and reports, giving the land its long name, that "The Luxuriant-Reed-Plains-the-Land-of-Fresh-Rice-ears-of-a-Thousand-Autumns,—of Long-Five-Hundred-Autumns is painfully uproarious,—it is." The gods met "in a divine assembly in the bed of the Tranquil River of Heaven", eighty of them in the Nihongi's count and eight hundred myriad in the Kojiki's, and the Nihongi gives the question that Taka-mi-musubi, the High Producing god, put to them: "I desire to have the evil Gods of the Central Land of Reed-Plains expelled and subdued. Whom is it meet that we should send for this purpose?"

Nihongi II, tr. Aston 1896 · Kojiki XXX, tr. Chamberlain 1882

The first envoy made friends with the god who held the land, Ōkuninushi, the Great Land Master, and sent no word for three years. The second, Ame-no-Wakahiko, the Young Prince of Heaven, married into it. "This God also was disloyal, and as soon as he arrived took to wife Shita-teru-hime, the daughter of Utsushi-kuni-dama", and Utsushi-kuni-dama, the Spirit of the Living Land, is the Great Land Master under another name, so the envoy had married the daughter of the god he was sent to subdue. He said what he wanted: "I, too, wish to govern the Central Land of Reed-Plains." The Kojiki puts his conduct in one line, that he "married Ōkuninushi's daughter Shitateru-hime, schemed to take the land, and for eight years did not report". The Nihongi adds, "He never reported the result of his mission." Heaven sent a pheasant to ask him, "Why for eight years bringest thou back no report?", and the Young Prince shot it. The arrow went on up into heaven, where the High Producing god knew it for the arrow he had given him, "It is stained with blood", and "took up the arrow and flung it back down (to earth)". It found the Young Prince lying down "after the feast of first-fruits" and killed him where he lay, which the Nihongi gives as the origin of a saying still used: "Fear a returning arrow."

Nihongi II, tr. Aston 1896 · Kojiki XXXI, tr. Chamberlain 1882 · Kojiki, as Unit 7 gives it

Then heaven sent force. Two warrior gods went down, the Great Land Master gave up the visible world and withdrew into the hidden one (Chapter I told it), and the land was cleared: "The two Gods at length put to death the malignant Deities and the tribes of herbs, trees and rocks. When all had been subdued, the only one who refused submission was the Star-God Kagase-wo." He is Ame no Kagase-wo, and under his other name Ama-tsu-mika-hoshi, the august star of heaven; the scholar Hirata Atsutane, in the early nineteenth century, took him for the planet Venus, and the book's Names table marks that reading unverified. Aston added a note on how little the books have to say for him: "This is the only Star-God mentioned in Japanese myth, and it may be noted that little honour is shown him." He goes on: "He is described as a conquered rebel, and has neither Kami nor Mikoto affixed to his name." Kami, god, and Mikoto, augustness, are the honorifics every other god in the two books is given. Another telling in the Nihongi moves the same star into heaven, where he had to die before anyone could go down at all: "In Heaven there is an Evil Deity called Ama-tsu-mika-hoshi, or Ame no Kagase-wo. We pray that this Deity may be executed before we go down to make clear the Central Land of Reed-Plains."

Nihongi II, tr. Aston 1896, with Aston's note

Only then did the king come. "Thereupon the August Grandchild, leaving the Heavenly rock-seat, and thrusting apart the eight-piled clouds of Heaven, clove his way with an awful way-cleaving, and descended from Heaven." Awful here means fearsome. The Kojiki has him "set off floating shut up in the Floating Bridge of Heaven, and descended from Heaven onto the peak of Kuzhifuru which is Takachiho in Tsukushi", a mountain on the southern island of Kyūshū. At the crossroads of heaven an earthly god stood waiting with a light and gave his name: "I am an Earthly Deity named the Deity Prince of Saruta." He had come, he said, because "I have come humbly to meet him and respectfully offer myself as His Augustness's vanguard." The grandson is Ninigi, and Japan's emperors descend from him.

Nihongi II, tr. Aston 1896 · Kojiki XXXIII, tr. Chamberlain 1882

Ethiopia's angels asked to go, and were told the sentence before they went. The Kebra Nagast, the Glory of the Kings, compiled in Ge'ez, the church language of Ethiopia, in the fourteenth century, is the book of Solomon, the Queen of Sheba and their son, and its hundredth chapter has angels volunteer: "And now try us well, and put us to the test so that Thou mayest know whether we are able to keep Thy word". God answered with the terms: "If now ye go astray so far as this in transgressing My word, the wrong will be upon your own heads", and "ye shall be kept in chains which can neither be loosened nor broken for ever." They went anyway, and the book says what they were given and what they went to: "there were given unto them with His word flesh, and blood, and a heart of the children of men. And they were content to leave the height of heaven, and they came down to earth, to the folly of the dancing of the children of Cain with all their work of the artisan, which they had made in the folly of their fornication, and to their singings, which they accompanied with the tambourine, and the flutes, and the pipes". The children of Cain are the line of the first murderer, who in this telling had filled the plain with music and the work of craftsmen. The book keeps the end too: God "bound them in the terror of Sheol [the Hebrew underworld] until the day of redemption".

Kebra Nagast 100, tr. Budge 1922, pp. 185-188

Iran put the going down to the souls as a question. The Bundahishn, the Zoroastrian book of creation put together in the ninth or tenth century AD from older teaching, says that before the evil spirit's attack Ohrmazd, the Wise Lord, asked the fravashis, the souls of men before their birth: "Which seems to you the more advantageous, when I shall present you to the world? that you shall contend in a bodily form with the fiend (druj), and the fiend shall perish, and in the end I shall have you prepared again perfect and immortal, and in the end give you back to the world, and you will be wholly immortal, undecaying, and undisturbed; or that it be always necessary to provide you protection from the destroyer?" They chose to go: "Thereupon, the guardian spirits of men became of the same opinion with the omniscient wisdom about going to the world". In Iran the descent is a volunteering for war, with the way back written into the offer.

The Jewish books of Enoch tell it as a desertion with a date and a place. 1 Enoch, whose first part, the Book of the Watchers, Watchers being its name for the angels who came down, was written in Aramaic in Judaea in the third century BC and survives whole only in Ge'ez, gives the number: "And they were in all two hundred; who descended [in the days] of Jared on the summit of Mount Hermon, and they called it Mount Hermon, because they had sworn and bound themselves by mutual imprecations upon it." The mountain has as many names as the Watchers, and the point of the names is a single one: the mountain the angels swore on in Enoch is, on one scholar's reading, a mountain that kings had sworn by a thousand years before. It stands where Lebanon, Syria and Israel now meet. The Hittites, whose kingdom of Hatti lay in what is now Turkey in the second millennium BC and whose treaties Chapter I read, called their mountains to witness the oaths of their kings, and Albrecht Goetze, who translated those treaties, took one of the mountains named in them, Šariyana, for Hermon; if he was right, this mountain was among the witnesses. Who the sons of God were

The first time the Bible shows the sons of God, the earth is being built and they are shouting. In the book of Job, God answers Job out of the whirlwind and asks where he was when the foundations were laid, "When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" The Hebrew is "כָּל־בְּנֵ֥י אֱלֹהִֽים", all the bene elohim; the Latin Bible has "omnes filii Dei"; and the Septuagint, the Greek translation made for the Jews of Alexandria in the last centuries BC, had already decided what they were: "When the stars were made, all my angels praised me with a loud voice." Genesis, when it names them, says less than Job. Its sixth chapter opens, "And it came to pass, when men began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters were born unto them", and then "the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair". Genesis says nothing of where the sons of God came from and nothing of their coming down. Everything after is what readers supplied, and the book lays it out as variant tellings, not as agreement. The ledger has the sons of God told by 110 passages across 19 archives, and of their coming down the ledger has tellings in archive after archive and Genesis has none. Their name runs through the tongues: bene elohim and bene ha-elohim in Hebrew, filii Dei in Augustine's Latin, bene ravrevaya, the sons of the great ones, in the Aramaic of the Targum, the synagogue's rendering of the Torah, Dieva dēli in Latvian, and in Estonian the famous sons of Vanaisa, the Old Father.

The Sibyl gave them a trade and a body. The first book of the Sibylline Oracles, written by Jews and Christians under the name of the Greek prophetess, makes them a race of inventors: "God formed another very subtile race / That cared for lovely works, and noble toils", who learned every art, and were "Industrious, worthy of that eponym", because they had a sleepless mind within and a huge body; the eponym, the name given them for it, is Watchers, the wakeful ones. The verse ends on their address: "but, notwithstanding, down they went / Into Tartarean chamber terrible". Tartarus is the Greek pit beneath the underworld. The Sibyl's Watchers go down after their inventions, not before, and what they taught is Chapter III's.

Sibylline Oracles 1.87-103, tr. Terry 1899

One archive says the sons of God were men, and that their height was a mountain. The Cave of Treasures, a Syriac history of the world from Adam to Christ written in the sixth or early seventh century, puts the children of Seth, Adam's third son, on the holy mountain beside Paradise, apart from the children of Cain in the plain below, and calls them sons of God for their purity. Their fathers had bound them by oath never to go down, and Jared commanded them to keep it. When some were drawn down by the music and the women of Cain's line, Enoch gave the sentence: "Hearken, O ye children of Seth, no man who shall transgress the commandment of Yârêd, and [break] the oaths of our fathers, and go down from this mountain, shall never again ascend it." The ones who went down looked back and found the way shut, and the book says how: "the stones of that holy mountain became fire in their sight, and having defiled their souls with the fire of fornication, God did not permit them to ascend to that holy place." No one locked the mountain; the stones turned to fire in the sight of the ones who had wanted what was below it.

The other reading makes them angels who came down, and the Bible has one such being come down on its own account. In the book of Daniel, written in the second century BC, King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon dreams that a watcher, a holy one, comes down from heaven and passes sentence on him, and watcher there is ʿir, the Aramaic word the Watchers carry. A Syriac chronicler, Syriac being the language of the Christians of Syria and Iraq, set both readings on one mountain and counted. Bar Hebraeus, the maphrian of the Syriac Orthodox Church, its second-ranking prelate, who wrote a history of the world about 1280, following a lost chronicle by Annianus, a monk of Alexandria of about 412, says the Sethites, some two hundred of them, "went up into the mountain of HERMON", and came down from it "in the fortieth year [of the life] of YARD, that is to say in the year one thousand of the world", and when they were down among the children of Cain they "set up over them the first king, a man whose name was SAMYAZOS". Samyazos is Shemihazah, the leader of the two hundred in 1 Enoch, so in this telling the first king on earth wore a Watcher's name. Bar Hebraeus gives the Sethites both titles, "'IRE (i.e. Watchers), and Sons of ALOHIM", and Michael the Syrian, a patriarch of the same church writing some ninety years before him, had read Annianus the same way, in Jean-Baptiste Chabot's French: "Annianus raconte d'eux qu'ils descendirent du mont Hermôn", Annianus tells of them that they came down from Mount Hermon. The two hundred who went up Hermon as men came down it under the name Enoch gave to angels.

Two more readers kept the law that held them up and the verb that brought them down. Al-Ṭabarī, the historian of Baghdad who died in 923, keeps the law that held them up there and gives it to Idris, the Qur'an's name for Enoch, who "commanded them ... not to mix with the descendants of Cain". Augustine, bishop of Hippo in North Africa, writing the City of God between about 417 and 426, put the two titles and the one descent into a single Latin sentence: "Spiritu Dei quippe fuerant facti angeli Dei et filii Dei, sed declinando ad inferiora dicuntur homines". In our English, by the Spirit of God they had been made angels of God and sons of God, but by declining to lower things they are called men. Declinando, by bending down, is this chapter's verb turned into a word about conduct.

The Jews' own translators turned the title toward rank and toward the sky. The Targum Onkelos, the Aramaic rendering of the Torah read in the synagogues of Babylonia from the early centuries AD, calls them the sons of the great ones, bene ravrevaya, which a modern English gives as "the sons of the rulers". Genesis Rabbah, the rabbis' commentary on Genesis compiled in Palestine about the fifth century, asks the question outright: "And why does it call them sons of God? Rabbi Hanina and Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish both say: because they multiplied days without pain and without sufferings. Rabbi Hana in the name of Rabbi Yose said: in order to stand upon the cycles (tequfot) and upon the calculations (heshbonot)". The tequfot are the year's turning points and the heshbonot the reckonings of the calendar: the rabbis' sons of God were long-lived men who kept the sky's count.

Hawaiʻi puts a woman, a man and a god in one era of births. Thrown by the foot

A fall from heaven takes one whole day, and the smith of the gods timed it. Hephaistos remembers in the Iliad the day he tried to defend his mother Hera from Zeus: "he caught me by the foot and hurled me from the heavenly threshold; the whole day long was I borne headlong, and at set of sun I fell in Lemnos, and but little life was in me." Lemnos is an island in the northern Aegean, and its people took him in: "There did the Sintian folk make haste to tend me for my fall." He had fallen once before, as an infant, thrown by his mother for his lameness, and two sea goddesses hid him in a cave under the stream of Ocean: "With them then for nine years' space I forged much cunning handiwork, brooches, and spiral arm-bands, and rosettes and necklaces, within their hollow cave". The other god Zeus threw was Ate, the goddess of blind folly, who had deceived him: "forthwith he seized Ate by her bright-tressed head, wroth in his soul, and sware a mighty oath that never again unto Olympus and the starry heaven should Ate come, she that blindeth all. So said he, and whirling her in his hand flung her from the starry heaven, and quickly she came to the tilled fields of men." She was sworn out, and she has lived among men since. The smith fell to an island and was nursed; folly fell to the fields and stayed.

Iliad 1.590-594, 18.395-405, tr. Murray 1924, 1925 · Iliad 19.126-131, tr. Murray 1925

Plato, the Athenian philosopher of the fourth century BC, would not let the smith's fall into his ideal city, and named it among the stories to be banned, "how on another occasion Zeus sent him flying for taking her part when she was being beaten", and he would not hear the excuse, "whether they are supposed to have an allegorical meaning or not". John Milton, two thousand years later, took the same fall and gave it to a fallen angel. In Paradise Lost of 1667 the smith is one of the rebel host under his Latin name Mulciber, and the Greek story is the pagans' garbled memory of the war in heaven: "from morn To noon he fell, from noon to dewy eve, A summer's day, and with the setting sun Dropt from the zenith, like a falling star, On Lemnos, th' Aegaean isle. Thus they relate, Erring; for he with this rebellious rout Fell long before". A rout is a rabble. Homer's day of falling and the angels' fall are one fall in Milton, and the equation is his.

Enoch's fallen are stars, and the first of them eats. The Book of Enoch tells the Watchers' coming a second time as a dream in which mankind is cattle, in the part called the Animal Apocalypse, which R. H. Charles dated to the war of Judas Maccabee, the Jewish leader who fought the Greek kings of Syria, 165 to 161 BC: "behold a star fell from heaven, and it arose and eat and pastured amongst those oxen", eat being the old past tense, and then "I saw many stars descend and cast themselves down from heaven to that first star, and they became bulls amongst those cattle and pastured with them". The first star fell; the rest cast themselves down after it, to where it was eating. Another chapter of the book, as Giorgio de Santillana and Hertha von Dechend print it in Hamlet's Mill, their book of 1969 on myth and the sky, makes the fallen stars the ones that "did not rise at their appointed time".

Egypt saw two stars fall and wrote both down. In Phrygia, in the uplands of what is now Turkey, a stone came down. The image of the Mother of the Gods at Pessinus was fetched to Rome in 204 BC, in the war with Hannibal, on the advice of the Sibylline Books, the prophecies Rome kept and consulted in emergencies, a different thing from the Jewish oracles under the Sibyl's name in the last section, and the historian Herodian, writing in Greek about AD 240, says of it: "The image itself they say fell from heaven; neither its material nor any craftsman who made it is known, and no human hand may touch it. The adversary of the Jewish and Christian tellings chose to go down and was thrown, and he has a name in every book. The Russian Primary Chronicle, compiled at Kiev about 1113, gives the speech of a Greek scholar sent to convert Prince Vladimir in 986, who tells him of the angel who said in his heart: "I shall descend to the earth, and seize upon it. I shall then be like to God, and shall establish my throne upon the northern clouds". The chronicle names him: "The name of this adversary was Sathanael". The words the scholar gives him are those of Isaiah 14, where the prophet has the king of Babylon say he will set his throne above the stars of God in the north; Isaiah's own words wait for the chapter Claimed. 2 Enoch has God tell Enoch of one of the angels who "conceived an impossible thought, to place his throne higher than the clouds above the earth, that he might become equal in rank to my power. And I threw him out from the height with his angels, and he was flying in the air continuously above the bottomless." Origen, the Christian teacher of Alexandria, writing about 230, cites Jesus in the Gospel of Luke for the same fall, "Behold, I see Satan fallen from heaven like lightning.", and draws the lesson, "For at one time he was light." The Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer, a Hebrew retelling of Genesis of about the eighth century, calls him Sammael and gives him a rank by his wings: "Sammael was the great prince in heaven; the Chajjôth had four wings and the Seraphim had six wings, and Sammael had twelve wings. What did Sammael do? He took his band and descended". The Chajjôth are the living creatures of Ezekiel's vision of the throne. Sathanael, Satanail, Satan, Sammael: in every one of these books he says what he wants, the earth and a throne over it, before he goes.

At Ugarit, the port on the Syrian coast whose clay tablets of the thirteenth century BC keep the poems of its gods, one god came down because the throne was too big for him, and nobody punished him. In the Baal cycle, after the storm god Baal dies, the gods look for a king to sit in his place and choose ʿAthtar, called the Terrible, who goes up to Baal's throne on the mountain Zaphon and sits on it; his feet do not reach the footstool and his head does not reach the top. He says he cannot be king on Zaphon, comes down from the throne, and becomes king over the earth. The tablets give him no fall and no sentence; he was the wrong size, and went down to where the size fit.

Inana went down past the earth, and hers is the one descent in this chapter that reaches the dead and comes back. The Sumerian poem of Inana's Descent, known from tablets of about 1800 BC, begins by saying three times where her attention went: "From the great above she set her ear toward the great below. The goddess, from the great above, set her ear toward the great below. Inana, from the great above, set her ear toward the great below." She is Inana in Sumerian and Ishtar in Akkadian, the queen of heaven of Sumer, the goddess of love and war and of the planet Venus; the great below is the land of the dead, where her sister Ereshkigal rules, whom the Akkadian texts also call Allatu. A Sumerian liturgy that Stephen Langdon printed in 1914 keeps the warning she was given and her answer to it: "O Innini, go not; the queen of the great house Not should'st thou know; not should'st thou enter.", and "A queen am I", and "the maiden went, to the darkness went". At the gate she called: "Open the house, doorman, open the house. Open the house, Neti, open the house: alone, I would come in." At each of seven gates she gave up something she wore, and before her sister she was struck dead and hung up: "The sick woman was turned into a piece of beaten meat; the piece of beaten meat was hung from a peg." Only "When three days and three nights had passed" did help come, from Enki, the god of the waters below, after Enlil, the chief of the gods, and Nanna, the moon, had refused, by two creatures he made for the purpose, and they brought her up.

Babylon and Assyria told it in Akkadian, from copies in the library of Nineveh of the seventh century BC. "Toward the land of No-Return, the region of darkness, Ishtar, the daughter of the Moon-god, directed her attention." The road is described as a house, "To the house from which none who enter come forth again," and "To the road whose course returns not", where "dust is their bread, and mud their food", and the dead "are clothed like birds in a garment of feathers". At the gate she threatened the dead on the living, "I will raise up the dead, that they may devour the living". At the first of the seven gates the porter took "the great crown from off her head", at the seventh "the garment covering the shame of her body", and to each protest he gave the same answer, in L. W. King's English of 1899: "Enter, O Lady, for thus are the laws of Allatu." Allatu herself, the queen below, was found weeping for what she kept: "I weep for the men who have left their wives." The way down to the dead was a house with seven doors and a law at each, and Inana went through all seven and came back up through them.

Seven gates down and seven up: of everyone in this section the one who went deepest is the one who came back, and the price of her return was fixed at the gates on the way down.

The finger in the savoury earth

The first thing anyone ever ate was the earth, and the sun and the moon appeared only because the eaters stopped shining. The Aggañña Sutta, whose elected king the section The crown before the king met, tells the descent from the inside and nearly whole, and this section lets it. When a world passes away, it says, "beings have mostly been reborn in the World of Radiance ; and there they dwell, made of mind, feeding on rapture, self-luminous, traversing the air, continuing in glory ; and thus they remain for a long, long period of time." When the world forms again, "beings who had deceased from the World of Radiance", the discourse says, usually "come to life as humans." The Pali gives their five marks in five words, "manomayā pītibhakkhā sayaṁpabhā antalikkhacarā subhaṭṭhāyino", and the translators, Thomas and Caroline Rhys Davids, husband and wife, who put the discourse into English in 1921, remark in a note that the description of them is scarcely human. Another discourse of the same canon, the Saṅgīti, keeps the sound those beings make: "There are beings who are soaked and steeped in happiness, full of it, pervaded by it. They from time to time pour forth ecstatic utterance saying : ' Oh the bliss of it ! Ah what happiness !' Such are the Radiant Devas".

Aggañña Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 27.10, tr. Rhys Davids 1921; Pali via SuttaCentral · Saṅgīti Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 33, tr. Rhys Davids 1921

Then the earth came to them, and the discourse describes it as a cook would: "And to those beings, Vāseṭṭha, sooner or later after a long time, earth with its savour was spread out in the waters. Even as a scum forms on the surface of boiled milky rice that is cooling, so did the earth appear." It had a taste: "even as the flawless honey of the bee, so sweet was it." One being did what the whole chapter turns on. "Then, Vāseṭṭha, some being of greedy disposition, said : Lo now! what will this be? and tasted the savoury earth with his finger. He thus, tasting, became suffused with the savour, and craving entered into him. And other beings, following his example, tasted the savoury earth with their finger." The commentator Buddhaghosa, in the fifth century AD, explained the greed, and the translators pass it on: "Greedy in his previous birth, remarks the Comy." Comy. is their shorthand for the commentary. They went from fingers to handfuls, "breaking off lumps of it with their hands", and from "the doing thereof the self-luminance of those beings faded away. As their self-luminance faded away, the moon and the sun became manifest." Night and day, months and seasons and years followed, because there was now a sun to count them by.

Aggañña Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 27.11-12, tr. Rhys Davids 1921, with the translators' note on Buddhaghosa

The weight came with the food: "And in measure as they thus fed, did their bodies become solid, and did variety in their comeliness become manifest. Some beings were well favoured, some were ill favoured." The handsome despised the plain, and the savoury earth went away, and they cried after it: "Alas for the savour ! alas for the savour !" The Buddha tells his listener that the cry is still in use: "Even so now when men having gotten a good savour say : Ah, the savour of it ! ah, the savour of it ! they do but follow an ancient primordial saying, not recognizing the significance thereof." When the first lusts had shamed them they built the first houses, "to conceal just that immorality." And when, long after, they meet to divide the rice, they remember what they were: "For in the past, we were made of mind, we fed on rapture, self-luminous, we traversed the air in abiding loveliness ; long long the period we so remained."

Aggañña Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 27.13, 17, 18, tr. Rhys Davids 1921

The Hermetic books, the Greek writings of the first centuries AD under the name of Hermes Thrice-Greatest from which the Asclepius came, tell a loss of the same shape, and tell it as a love story. The first of them, the Poimandres, put into English by G. R. S. Mead in 1906, makes Man the Father's image: "But All-Father Mind, being Life and Light, did bring forth Man co-equal to Himself, with whom He fell in love, as being His own child; for he was beautiful beyond compare, the Image of his Sire." Man asked leave to make, and "assent was given him by the Father." He passed down through the seven spheres of the planets, which the book calls the Harmony, and the rulers of the spheres "fell in love with him, and gave him each a share of his own ordering." Then he leaned over the edge: "bent his face downwards through the Harmony, breaking right through its strength, and showed to downward Nature God’s fair Form." In plain words, he bent down through the seven spheres and showed Nature his face. Nature saw him in her water, "for ’twas as though she’d seen the image of Man’s fairest form upon her Water, his shadow on her Earth." And the wanting ran both ways: "He in his turn beholding the form like to himself, existing in her, in her Water, loved it and willed to live in it; and with the will came act, and [so] he vivified the form devoid of reason." Then "Nature took the object of her love and wound herself completely round him, and they were intermingled, for they were lovers."

Poimandres (Corpus Hermeticum I) 12-14, tr. Mead 1906

From that embrace, the book says, comes the double nature of everyone alive: "And this is why beyond all creatures on the earth man is twofold; mortal because of body, but because of the essential Man immortal." The price is stated once: "Thus though above the Harmony, within the Harmony he hath become a slave." But the Poimandres keeps the road up open, and gives the toll at each gate. At death "man doth speed his way thereafter upwards through the Harmony", and at each sphere he hands back what he took on the way down: "To the first zone he gives the Energy of Growth and Waning; unto the second [zone], Device of Evils [now] de-energized; unto the third, the Guile of the Desires de-energized; unto the fourth, his Domineering Arrogance, [also] de-energized". Stripped of all seven he "cometh to that Nature which belongs unto the Eighth, and there with those-that-are hymneth the Father." At the end of an age the rule that binds the spheres' makers is lifted too: "The period being ended, the bond that bound them all was loosened by God's Will." The book ends with its teacher calling to the ones who have not started back: "Ye earth-born folk, why have ye given up yourselves to Death, while yet ye have the power of sharing Deathlessness?" Inana paid at seven gates going down and took her payment back coming up; the Poimandres pays at seven spheres going up. No source sets the two sevens together, and the setting is ours ◇.

Poimandres 15, 18, 25-26, 28, tr. Mead 1906

Another Hermetic book, the Virgin of the World, in which the goddess Isis teaches her son Horus what came down to her from Hermes, tells the going down as a sentence passed on souls. The souls made in heaven "began to overstep their proper limits and their reservations, and would no longer stay in the same place, but were for ever moving, and thought that being ever stationed in one place was death." So "the Sovereign King of all resolved to fabricate with art the human frame, in order that in it the race of Souls throughout might be chastised." Hermes made the frame weak on purpose, "so that the plasm was in every way relaxable, and weak and powerless, in order that it might not, in addition to its natural sagacity, be full of power as well." The souls heard it: "Then were they first plunged in deep gloom, and, learning that they were condemned, began to wail." Some "hissed like snakes"; one spoke for them all, of the eyes they were to get, "Windows they are, not eyes!" and of the home: "For home, instead of this great world high in the air, a heart’s small mass awaits us." Then the complaint every prisoner makes: "appoint for us some limits!"

The Virgin of the World (Korē Kosmou) 15, 18-19, 21, tr. Mead 1906

God answered them with a law, and the law has the way back written into it. "O Souls, Love and Necessity shall be your lords, they who are lords and marshals after Me of all." Souls without fault stay in the fields of heaven; the guilty "shall dwell in the place that Destiny allots, condemned to mortal wombs." For slight faults they "shall again embrace your [father] Heaven, and sigh no more", and the best of them are promised offices on earth: they "shall among men be righteous kings, and genuine philosophers, founders of states, and lawgivers, and real seers". The souls who came down as prisoners may rule as kings, and the section Thus you shall be called Thoth follows that office. As the souls entered the bodies a great spirit rose from the earth and asked Hermes what the new things were called, and when he said men, gave its verdict on the whole business: "it is a daring work, this making man, with eyes inquisitive, and talkative of tongue". Another Hermetic piece, the Sacred Sermon, gives the rule of the whole traffic in a phrase: "every soul infleshed by revolution of the Cyclic Gods", the souls put into flesh by the turning of the planets.

The Virgin of the World 22-23, 25, tr. Mead 1906 · The Sacred Sermon (Corpus Hermeticum III) 3, tr. Mead 1906

The Gnostics, Christians of the second century who held that this world was made and ruled by lesser powers, told the same descent of a woman. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon about 180, reports the teaching of Simon of Samaria, the magician of the Acts of the Apostles, who said that a woman he had bought out of slavery at Tyre, Helena, was "the first conception of his mind, the mother of all". She came down and could not go back: "For this Ennœa leaping forth from him, and comprehending the will of her father, descended to the lower regions [of space], and generated angels and powers, by whom also he declared this world was formed." Ennoia is Greek for Thought. Her own creations held her, "so that she could not return upwards to her father, but was even shut up in a human body, and for ages passed in succession from one female body to another, as from vessel to vessel." One of her bodies, Simon said, was "that Helen on whose account the Trojan War was undertaken". The Gnostic rulers themselves are under the weight. The Apocryphon of John, a book of the second century found at Nag Hammadi in Egypt, says that through them "bitter fate was begotten" and that "they are bound with measures and times and moments". The Pistis Sophia, a Coptic Gnostic book of the third century, has the Watchers' story told of the rulers and the sky together. In plain words first: the rulers of the sky under their tyrant Adamas rebelled and bred more powers, and a higher power, Yew, came out from the side of the Light, which the book calls the Right, and bound them to the circle of fate, the ring of the planets; the decans are the thirty-six divisions of the zodiac. The book's words: "When the rulers of Adamas mutinied and persistently practised congress, procreating rulers, archangels, angels, servitors and decans, ... Yew, the father of my father, came forth from the Right and bound them to a Fate-sphere". And one Gnostic woman answers the rulers from above: Norea, in the Hypostasis of the Archons, from the same Egyptian jars, tells them "it is from the world above that I am come".

Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.23.2, ANF · Apocryphon of John II 28, tr. Wisse (NHL) · Pistis Sophia 136, tr. Mead 1921 · Hypostasis of the Archons 92, tr. Layton (NHL)

The Zohar, the book of the Jewish mystics written in Castile at the end of the thirteenth century, says the same of two of the Watchers, Aza and Azael, Azael being a form of Azazel, one of the leaders of the two hundred: "And these, Aza and Azael, who rebelled above, and the Holy One, blessed be He, cast them down, and they took bodily form in the earth and were sustained in it and could not strip themselves of it." They are there still: "And until now, this very day, they exist and teach sorceries to the sons of men". The Kebra Nagast's angels were fitted with "flesh, and blood, and a heart of the children of men" before they went, and the Zohar's could not take the flesh off after. The body they put on to come down was a body they could not take off. Korea knew the difficulty from the other side. In the Samguk yusa Hwanung, who came down with three thousand, could not at first rule the humans he had come for, and Homer Hulbert, who retold the record in 1905, says why: he had not yet taken human shape, and found it hard to take control of a human kingdom without one. The Old Record says that a bear and a tiger prayed to the god to be made human, and that the bear, who kept the fast of mugwort and garlic he set them and stayed out of the sunlight, was given a woman's body. When the bear who had become a woman prayed for a child under the altar tree, the god took on a borrowed form to marry her, and she bore Dangun.

Zohar I:58a, our rendering · Kebra Nagast 100, tr. Budge 1922 · Samguk yusa 1 and Hulbert 1905, as the East and the star file gives them, in our words

India's monks, Egypt's Greek writers, the Gnostics and the Zohar never compare notes in our files, and the agreement among them is ours to point out ◇. In each of them the weight comes in by the same door: a finger in the earth, a face in the water, a thing made and loved, a body put on to rule; and in each the light goes out at the moment of the wanting and does not come back on. In every one of these records the first thing paid was light, and it was paid for something below that could be tasted or held.

By lot, and over a bull's blood

The kings of Atlantis hunted their bull with staves and nooses and killed it over the writing of the law. Plato's Critias, the unfinished dialogue in which an Athenian named Critias passes on what the priests of Egypt told the lawgiver Solon, begins the island's history with the gods' lots: "I have before remarked in speaking of the allotments of the gods, that they distributed the whole earth into portions differing in extent, and made for themselves temples and instituted sacrifices. And Poseidon, receiving for his lot the island of Atlantis, begat children by a mortal woman". The woman was Cleito, the only daughter of an earth-born man, and the god enclosed her hill with rings of sea and land, "there were two of land and three of water, which he turned as with a lathe". Then the count: "He also begat and brought up five pairs of twin male children; and dividing the island of Atlantis into ten portions, he gave to the first-born of the eldest pair his mother's dwelling and the surrounding allotment, which was the largest and best, and made him king over the rest". He named them: "the eldest, who was the first king, he named Atlas, and after him the whole island and the ocean were called Atlantic." The second twin held the end of the island toward Gades, Cádiz in Spain, and Plato gives his name in two languages: "he gave the name which in the Hellenic language is Eumelus, in the language of the country which is named after him, Gadeirus." Eight more names follow, and the line of the eldest kept the kingship, "the eldest son handing it on to his eldest for many generations".

Plato, Critias 113c-114c, tr. Jowett

The ten kings ruled under a written law, and the law was sworn in blood. "Now the order of precedence among them and their mutual relations were regulated by the commands of Poseidon which the law had handed down. These were inscribed by the first kings on a pillar of orichalcum, which was situated in the middle of the island, at the temple of Poseidon, whither the kings were gathered together every fifth and every sixth year alternately, thus giving equal honour to the odd and to the even number." Orichalcum is a metal Plato says the island dug out of its ground, and the Greeks of his day thought it lost. There they "hunted the bulls, without weapons, but with staves and nooses; and the bull which they caught they led up to the pillar and cut its throat over the top of it so that the blood fell upon the sacred inscription. Now on the pillar, besides the laws, there was inscribed an oath invoking mighty curses on the disobedient." They mixed a clot of blood for each of them into wine and drank, and swore to judge by the writing and to "neither command others, nor obey any ruler who commanded them, to act otherwise than according to the laws of their father Poseidon." Then, "when darkness came on, and the fire about the sacrifice was cool, all of them put on most beautiful azure robes, and, sitting on the ground, at night, over the embers of the sacrifices by which they had sworn, and extinguishing all the fire about the temple, they received and gave judgment", and "at daybreak they wrote down their sentences on a golden tablet". The first law of the ten was peace among themselves: "They were not to take up arms against one another, and they were all to come to the rescue if any one in any of their cities attempted to overthrow the royal house", and the last was a limit on the eldest: "And the king was not to have the power of life and death over any of his kinsmen unless he had the assent of the majority of the ten."

Plato, Critias 119c-120d, tr. Jowett

Plato gives the rule of the lots in two other places. In the Critias again: "In the days of old, the gods had the whole earth distributed among them by allotment", and "when they had peopled them they tended us, their nurselings and possessions, as shepherds tend their flocks, excepting only that they did not use blows or bodily force, as shepherds do, but governed us like pilots from the stern of the vessel, which is an easy way of guiding animals, holding our souls by the rudder of persuasion according to their own pleasure". The Statesman sets it in the reign of Kronos, the old king of the Greek gods and father of Zeus, whom the Romans called Saturn, when "There were demigods, who were the shepherds of the various species and herds of animals, and each one was in all respects sufficient for those of whom he was the shepherd; neither was there any violence, or devouring of one another, or war or quarrel among them". The chapter has now met two oaths sworn by companies of heaven's kin as they took the earth. On Hermon the two hundred "had sworn and bound themselves by mutual imprecations upon it"; at the pillar the ten kings swore under "an oath invoking mighty curses on the disobedient". Both bind the company together by a curse at the moment of taking. No source sets the mountain beside the pillar, and setting them together is ours ◇. The ledger has the sharing-out of the earth told by 35 passages across 14 archives.

Philo of Byblos, the Phoenician who wrote in Greek about AD 100 and whose succession of gods Chapter I followed, has Kronos, the Greek name he gives the Phoenician god El, hand out the earth himself, a city at a time, as a father portions his kin. "Kronos also, in going round the world, gives the kingdom of Attica to his own daughter Athena." Then the Phoenician cities: "Kronos gives the city Byblos to the goddess Baaltis, who is also called Dione, and Berytus to Poseidon and to the Cabeiri and Agrotae and Halieis". Berytus is Beirut; the Cabeiri are a company of gods Philo had named earlier, and the Agrotae and the Halieis are, in Greek, the Hunters and the Fishers. And the largest portion went to the god of letters: "And when Kronos came into the South country he gave all Egypt to the god Tauthus, that it might be his royal dwelling-place." This is the first entrance of the god the section Thus you shall be called Thoth is named for, and Philo gives his names in one breath: Tauthus in his Phoenician, called Thoyth by the Egyptians, Thoth by the Alexandrians and Hermes by the Greeks, the inventor of letters, who "gave form to the sacred characters of the letters" and devised for Kronos "as insignia of royalty four eyes in front and behind". The equation of the four names is Philo's own. Philo shares out the mountains too, to the giants of the first age: "They begot sons greater in size and stature, whose names were given to the mountains over which they ruled. Thus from them derive the names of Mount Kassios, the Lebanon, the Anti-Lebanon and Mount Brathy." Diodorus of Sicily, a Greek historian of the first century BC, says that after the death of Hyperion the children of Ouranos, the sky, divided the kingdom among themselves, and that the land by the ocean fell by lot to Atlas, who named its people and its greatest mountain after himself.

The Sibyl's third book, written by Jews in Egypt in the second or first century BC, has the same three brothers of Chapter I share the earth after the flood, "for there were oaths of the father and just portions". Babylon put the lots at the very beginning, before there were men to rule. The poem of Atra-ḫasīs, copied about 1650 BC, says of the gods: "They cast lots, the gods took their shares: / Anu went up to heaven"; Enlil took the earth and Enki the waters below. Egypt tells a division and a second thought. The Shabaka Stone, a black slab on which a king of Kush ruling Egypt about 710 BC had an older text copied because the original was worm-eaten, says that Geb, the earth god, first judged between Horus and Seth, the two gods who fought for Egypt's throne, this Seth being an Egyptian god and no kin to Adam's son, and gave each a half, and then "it seemed wrong to Geb that the portion of Horus was like the portion of Seth", and he gave the whole to Horus. At Dendera, as Chapter I told, the dead Osiris was himself shared across the land, one relic to a province. In India the gods shared out a goddess. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, a priests' manual of the first millennium BC, says that when Śrī, Fortune, came out of the creator shining, the gods wanted to kill her for what she had, and were told to take it and leave her alive: "Agni then took her food, Soma her royal power, Varuṇa her universal sovereignty, Mitra her noble rank, Indra her power, Bṛhaspati her holy lustre, Savitṛ her dominion, Pūṣan her wealth, Sarasvatī her prosperity, and Tvaṣṭṛ her beautiful forms."

The Norse gods shared the world with the giants and walled the middle off. Snorri Sturluson, the Icelander who wrote the Prose Edda about 1220, says that when the sons of Borr had made the earth of Ymir's body, "along the strand of that sea they gave lands to the races of giants for habitation. But on the inner earth they made a citadel round about the world against the hostility of the giants, and for their citadel they raised up the brows of Ymir the giant, and called that place Midgard." Midgard is the middle world of men. The Völuspá has the gods meet on what they are owed: "Then sought the gods their assembly-seats, / The holy ones, and council held, / Whether the gods should tribute give, / Or to all alike should worship belong." Britain was a giants' island before it was divided. Geoffrey of Monmouth, a Welsh cleric who wrote the History of the Kings of Britain about 1136, has the goddess Diana send Brutus, the Trojan whom Geoffrey makes Britain's founder, to it; in Aaron Thompson's verse of 1718: "Brutus! there lies beyond the Gallic bounds / An island which the western sea surrounds. / By giants once possess'd; now few remain / To bar thy entrance, or obstruct thy reign." When the Trojans came, "The island was then called Albion, and was inhabited by none but a few giants. ... They therefore passed through all the provinces, forced the giants to fly into the caves of the mountains, and divided the country among them according to the directions of their commander." On the north shore of the Black Sea a dead hero held an island and a land, a portion with a dead man for its lord. An oil flask of the fifth century BC from the White Island, where Thetis carried Achilles from his pyre, is dedicated by one Glaukos to him as "ruler of Leuke", Leuke being the island's Greek name, and Alcaeus, a poet of about 600 BC, in a line kept by one twelfth-century commentator, hailed him "Achilles, you who rule the Scythian [land]".

Some of the old holders would not share with men at all, and took terms. Thus you shall be called Thoth

The sun god named his own deputy before he left, and he gave the name with the job. In the Book of the Heavenly Cow, an Egyptian book painted in the royal tombs of the New Kingdom, Ra, grown old, withdraws from the earth into the sky, and before he goes up he turns to the god of the moon and of writing: "You are to be in my place, my vicar. Thus you shall be called Thoth, the vicar of Re". A vicar is a stand-in, and Re is Ra. The name is given with the office, and the one who held the office was called by the name. Thoth is Philo's Tauthus of the section before this, and the Greeks' Hermes. The ledger has a name that designates an office told by 42 passages across 16 archives.

The man who went up in the generation the Watchers came down carries the most names in the chapter. One man, many names: Enoch, Idris, Hermes, Atlas, Metatron. He is Ḥanokh in Hebrew, Henokh in Ge'ez and Idris in the Qur'an. Abū Maʿshar, the Persian astrologer of ninth-century Baghdad, taught that there had been three men called Hermes and that the first of them, who lived before the flood, was Enoch. The sentence that follows is his as later writers copied it, and it gives that first Hermes four names: "The Persians name him Wiwanghan (Awanjhan), that is, the Just, in their accounts of the kings; his father's father was Gayumart, that is, Adam; the Hebrews say he is Enoch, in Arabic Idris", Gayumart being the first man of the Persian books. Pseudo-Eupolemus, a Jewish writer in Greek of the second century BC whom Eusebius quotes, adds the Greek name, "But the Greeks say that Atlas invented astrology, and that Atlas is the same as Enoch", which the next chapter takes up; and 3 Enoch, below, makes him Metatron. The story-name is Enoch. Genesis gives him one verse: "And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him." His father is Jared, in whose days the two hundred came down. 2 Enoch has the Lord tell the archangel Michael what to do with the man who has come up: "Go and take from Enoch his earthly robe, and anoint him with My holy oil, and clothe him with the raiment of My glory", and Enoch looks at himself: "And I gazed upon myself, and I was like one of His glorious ones. And there was no difference, and fear and trembling departed from me." He was given the trade of the place, "and I wrote 366 books." The Ethiopian Synaxarium, the church's book of saints' days, keeps the day he went: "on this day they [angels] carried away Enoch, the son of Jared", "with the rushing of stars, and of lightnings and of winds, and they took him up above the heavens and seated him between two spiritual beings. And he is called the scribe of the commandments of God". On his way up through the heavens 2 Enoch has him pass the Grigori, but no record makes the son's going up and the angels' coming down one movement, and reading them as a crossing on the same road is ours ◇.

China gives the clearest case of a name handed from one holder to the next, with the day of the handing. Sima Qian, the court historian of the Han who finished his Records of the Historian, the Shiji, about 90 BC, writes in his history of the house of Chu of one officer under a double name: "Chong-Li held the office of Fire Regulator under Di Ku of Gaoxin; he had great merit and could make the world bright and warm, so Di Ku named him Zhurong. When the Gonggong clan rose in rebellion Di Ku sent Chong-Li to execute them, and he did not finish. So on a gengyin day the Lord executed Chong-Li, and set his younger brother Wuhui in Chong-Li's place, again as Fire Regulator, as Zhurong." The name was the office, and the office outlived the man by the length of an execution. Chong and Li are also the names of the two officers who, in the chapter The Cut, sever heaven from earth. Qu Yuan, the poet of Chu of the fourth century BC, opens his long poem the Li Sao with his own descent from the same house and his birth on the same day of the sixty-day cycle: "A far descendant of Lord Gaoyang; my august father was called Boyong. When Sheti stood true in the first month of spring, on the day gengyin I came down." Sheti is a group of stars by which the old calendar fixed the first month of the year. The Chinese verb for his birth is the verb for coming down. Sima Qian's gengyin is a day of execution and Qu Yuan's a day of birth; the match of the days is ours to point out ◇.

A star's name came down into a poet. Li Bai, the poet of the eighth century AD, was named for Taibai, the Grand White, which is the planet Venus, called Changgeng when it shines in the evening. The same historian's treatise on the heavens had already given the planet an office and thirteen other names: "Taibai is the great minister; its title is Supreme Duke. Its other names: Yin Star, Great Regulator, Camp Star, Watching Star, Palace Star, Bright Star, Great Decline, Great Marsh, Final Star, Great Minister, Heaven's Vastness, Order Star, Moon's Woof." It gives the star's rule for armies too: "When Taibai is hidden, to send out troops brings disaster on them." Li Bai's kinsman Li Yangbing wrote in 762 that on the night of his birth his mother dreamed of the star: "On the night of the birth-pangs, Changgeng entered the dream; so at birth he was named Bai, and styled Taibai." A later writer, Pei Jing, gives the reason for the name his friends gave him, Secretary He being the court poet He Zhizhang: "Some say: the essence of Taibai came down, hence the style Taibai, hence Secretary He called him the Banished Immortal." The essence of a star came down into a man, and the man was called by the star's office. Rome gave the same star two names by its two shifts. Pliny the Elder, who wrote his Natural History before he died at Vesuvius in AD 79, says of Venus: "it receives the name of Lucifer, as if it were another sun, hastening on the day. On the contrary, when it shines in the west, it is named Vesper, as prolonging the light, and performing the office of the moon." The Greeks let the dead of the first age keep an office. The way back

The records of this chapter count the travellers in both directions, and the count comes out even for a while. Zeus is back on Olympus "on the twelfth day". Tibet's first seven kings went up the cord they came down and left no bodies on the earth. Etana came down with the kingship and went up on an eagle; the sons of Heaven's light went out "along the light of the sun and moon" by the same smoke-hole it came in by. Inana set her ear toward the great below, hung three days and three nights on a peg, and came up through the seven gates. In the Bundahishn the souls went down with the return in the contract, "and in the end give you back to the world". And in the generation in which two hundred came down on Hermon, one man went up: "And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him." Others went up who had not come down, taken up and not back, and the records keep them on the same road. Diodorus has Hesperus, Atlas's son, go up Mount Atlas to watch the stars and be taken in a storm, and the evening star got his name. Hesiod has a boy taken: Phaethon, a son of the Dawn, "whom, when he was a young boy in the tender flower of glorious youth with childish thoughts, laughter-loving Aphrodite seized and caught up and made a keeper of her shrine by night, a divine spirit." Egypt has the god Seth, no kin to Adam's son, beaten by Horus, taken up by Ra "to dwell with me and be my son, and he shall thunder in the sky and be feared." And in the Book of Giants that Mani's church carried east, a giant's son, Mahaway, flies "like an eagle" across "the great desert" to Enoch, and hears Enoch's voice "from the south ... speaking my name very lovingly", and flies back. In the first age the door was a door, and opened both ways.
