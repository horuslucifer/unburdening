Moment 1 of 14

It will not hold under my horse’s feet

Armenia’s epic of Sasun says its last hero waits in the rock. David cursed his son Little Mher to be deathless and heirless, and gave him the Raven’s Rock for his place until the world is destroyed and built again, so that the ground can hold him. Russia’s giant Svyatogor carries the same weight: the damp mother earth would not bear him, and lifting a bag that held the earth’s pull he sank to his knees, blood instead of tears on his face. He breathes his strength into Ilya, who refuses more: I have strength enough, or the earth will not carry me.

Moment 2 of 14

This earth, the bearer, could not bear herself

The Mahābhārata says the Earth herself went up to the gods because the kings born on her were too many: this earth, the bearer, could not bear herself. Once no child died and the whole earth was filled thickly with living things, and when Yama, god of death, stopped killing at the gods’ sacrifice, the creatures multiplied past bearing. The Harivaṃśa’s Earth comes worn out by the burden like a ship, and Brahmā names the remedy, the taking-down of the burden: the kings must be killed to settle it. Al-Bīrūnī carried the plan into Arabic, that when the earth has too many inhabitants its ruler sends a messenger to reduce the number.

Greece says the same of its war. The Cypria has Zeus see the countless tribes of men oppressing the earth and resolve in pity to relieve her by the great struggle of the Ilian war. The old note on Homer that keeps those lines adds that Earth herself asked to be lightened, and that Momos gave Zeus his counsel. Euripides’ Apollo says the gods made deaths to draw off the overflowing fullness of mortals, and Achilles, sitting by the ships, calls himself a profitless burden upon the earth.

Elsewhere the Earth speaks for herself: Ovid’s Earth, scorched, lifts her face and complains to Jupiter, and in Nonnus she begs Zeus to look on the pains of the downcast world. How the ground came to be so heavy

Babylon’s Atra-ḫasīs says twelve hundred years had not yet gone by when the people grew many and the god was troubled by their clamour, and plague and famine came year by year before the flood. In Iran Yima, told the earth is full, enlarges it three times, and the dragon Azi Dahaka begs the river goddess for the boon of emptying all seven regions of men.

Its Man, though deathless and above the Harmony, suffers as a mortal and has become a slave within it. In the Virgin of the World, Hermes sets a spell on his hidden books before he returns to heaven.

The Virgin of the World (Hermetica)

Christian, Gnostic and Hermetic · 2nd–3rd century AD

A Hermetic treatise in Greek, kept in Stobaeus’s anthology, in which Isis tells Horus how the souls were made and put into bodies. English of G. R. S. Mead, 1906.

"[Hermes], ere he returned to Heaven, invoked a spell on them, and spake these words. (For ’tis not meet, my son, that I should leave this proclamation ineffectual, but [rather] should speak forth what words [our] Hermes uttered [p. 97] when he hid his books away.) Thus then he said:" "“O holy books, who have been made by my immortal hands, by incorruption’s magic spells, . . . free from decay throughout eternity remain and incorrupt from time! Become unseeable, unfindable, for every one whose foot shall tread the plains of this [our] land, until old Heaven doth bring forth meet instruments for you, whom the Creator shall call souls.”" "Thus spake he; and, laying spells on them by means of his own works, he shuts them safe away in their own zones. And long enough the time has been since they were hid away." Mead's note on the spell: "The text is here again hopeless. Meineke’s emendation (*Adnot*., p. cxxx.) ἃς . . . φαρμάκῳ χρίσας ἐπικρατῷ—which makes Hermes smear the books with some magical ointment—is ingenious, but hardly satisfactory, though Wachsmuth adopts it." Mead's note on the last sentence: "This is purely conjectural; the text is utterly corrupt."
