Moment 1 of 7

The records keep their own clocks

The records keep their own clocks: the rabbis give the world six thousand years, and the Zohar opens the gates of wisdom in the six hundredth year of the sixth millennium. The Bundahishn counts twelve thousand years in all, and the Bahman Yasht seven ages of metal. The Indian astronomer Āryabhaṭa counted the sun’s revolutions in an age and dated his work from the Bhārata war, the war in which Kṛṣṇa says, in Oppenheimer’s English, Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds. Darius counts eight of his race as kings before him, and the First Emperor of China counted his line forward to ten thousand generations.

Nine thousand years back to Atlantis

Plato’s Egyptian priest of Saïs, who told Solon that the Greeks were never anything but children, counted nine thousand years back to the war with Atlantis, and said many great deluges had come within those years. He said Athens was founded a thousand years before Saïs, and that Solon heard Greek names for foreign kings because the names had been translated as they crossed. Herodotus was told that in the long count of Egypt’s kings the sun had four times risen contrary to custom, twice rising where it now sets.

Moment 3 of 7

A natural age is the longest life

The Etruscans held that their nation had been given a set number of ages, each ended by a sign from heaven. Censorinus, writing in AD 238, defines the measure: a natural age is the longest duration of human life, bounded by birth and death. The portents were written down, and Rome took its hundred-year age from the Etruscans. Plutarch says a trumpet rang out of a clear sky at the end of one Etruscan age, and the haruspex Vulcanius told the assembly that Caesar’s comet marked the end of the ninth age and the start of the tenth.

Plutarch, Life of Sulla

Greece · about AD 100

Plutarch’s life of the Roman dictator, which tells of a trumpet heard from a clear sky and the Etruscan teaching that it marked the end of one age of the world and the start of another.

"But most important of all, out of a cloudless and clear air there rang out the voice of a trumpet, prolonging a shrill and dismal note, so that all were amazed and terrified at its loudness. The Tuscan wise men declared that the prodigy foretokened a change of conditions and the advent of a new age. For according to them there are eight ages in all, differing from one another in the lives and customs of men, and to each of these God has appointed a definite number of times and seasons, which is completed by the circuit of a great year. And whenever this circuit has run out, and another begins, some wonderful sign is sent from earth or heaven, so that it is at once clear to those who have studied such subjects and are versed in them, that men of other habits and modes of life have come into the world, who are either more or less of concern to the gods than their predecessors were." "All things, they say, undergo great changes, as one again succeeds another, and especially the art of divination; at one period it rises in esteem and is successful in its predictions, because manifest and genuine signs are sent forth from the Deity; and again, in another age, it is in small repute, being off-hand, for the most part, and seeking to grasp the future by means of faint and blind senses. Such, at any rate, was the tale told by the wisest of the Tuscans, who were thought to know much more about it than the rest."
