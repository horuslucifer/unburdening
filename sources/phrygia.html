'The things of Nannakos': the proverb is said of things wondered at for their age, or of people who lament a great deal. For Nannakos was king of the Phrygians, as Hermogenes says in his Phrygian History, before the times of Deucalion; foreseeing the flood that was coming, he gathered everyone into the sanctuaries and made supplication with tears.

in the story The Elders · Remedy · u11-0165

They say there was a certain Annakos who lived beyond three hundred years. The people round about asked the oracle how long he would live, and the answer was given that when he died all would be destroyed. The Phrygians, hearing it, lamented bitterly; hence the proverb 'to weep as over Annakos', of those who grieve exceedingly. When the flood came in Deucalion's time, all were destroyed. When the earth had dried, Zeus ordered Prometheus and Athena to mould images again out of the mud; and summoning the winds he ordered them to breathe into all of them, and made them living. Because the images (εἰκόνες) were shaped there, it was called Iconium.

“Apollo also slew Marsyas, the son of Olympus. For Marsyas, having found the pipes which Athena had thrown away because they disfigured her face, engaged in a musical contest with Apollo. They agreed that the victor should work his will on the vanquished, and when the trial took place Apollo turned his lyre upside down in the competition and bade Marsyas do the same.
